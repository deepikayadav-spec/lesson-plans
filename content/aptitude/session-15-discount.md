## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Define Marked Price and Discount%, and state that discount is always calculated on Marked Price, not Cost Price. *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Compute the discount% behind a real-world offer (e.g. "buy 3, get 1 free") and calculate the single discount equivalent to two or more successive discounts. *(APPLYING)*
3. Compare a successive-discount scheme against a flat discount to determine which actually gives the buyer the better price. *(ANALYZING)*
4. Compute the resulting profit or loss % when an article is marked up over Cost Price and then sold at a discount on Marked Price. *(APPLYING)*
5. Solve reverse discount problems — find a missing second discount, a required markup over Cost Price, or handle a partial/tiered discount — given the final Selling Price. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice on Session 14 (0–7 min)

7 questions, real numbers and real options from Session 14 (*Percentages & Profit/Loss — Company Specific Questions*). ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** Rohit Sharma scored 110 runs, including 3 boundaries and 8 sixes. What % of his total score came from running between the wickets?
`A` 54.50% · `B` 45.45% · `C` 58% · `D` 50%
→ **B.** *Targets:* isolating "runs from boundaries/sixes" then taking the remainder as a % of the *original* total, not a new base.

**Q2.** In the exam-marks chain question (Ram scored 10% less than Sham, Sham 25% more than Priya, Priya 20% less than Charan, exam out of 500, Ram scored 360), what % of the total did Charan score?
`A` 90% · `B` 80% · `C` 71.20% · `D` 75%
→ **B.** *Targets:* walking a chain of "X% more/less than" statements without losing track of which base each % is on — exactly the skill today's discount chains need.

**Q3.** Argentina's population is 294,000 (150,000 men); 53% of the total population can read/write, and 98% of men can. What % of *women* can read and write?
`A` 5.125% · `B` 6% · `C` 5.3% · `D` 6.125%
→ **D.** *Targets:* subtracting group totals (readers who can read/write minus male readers) instead of taking a % of the wrong base.

**Q4.** Samsung cut fridge prices by 25%, and sales rose by 20%. What happened to total revenue?
`A` Increased by 20% · `B` Increased by 10% · `C` Decreased by 10% · `D` Decreased by 20%
→ **C.** *Targets:* successive % changes **multiply** (0.75 × 1.20 = 0.90 → –10%), they do not add (–25 + 20 ≠ –10). **This is today's core mechanic — flag it and say you'll use it again in five minutes.**

**Q5.** The cost price of 20 articles equals the selling price of 10 articles. What is the profit or loss %?
`A` Gain 50% · `B` Gain 100% · `C` Loss 50% · `D` Loss 100%
→ **B.** *Targets:* setting CP of one article = 1 unit to compare ratios cleanly, rather than getting lost in the article counts.

**Q6.** An off-season discount of x% is offered, plus a further 12.5% off if the bill exceeds Rs 500. A shopper pays Rs 525 for jeans listed at Rs 750. What is x?
`A` 30% · `B` 25% · `C` 20% · `D` 35%
→ **C.** *Targets:* peeling off the *second* discount first to recover the price before it, then finding the first discount% from that. **This is a Discount problem already sitting inside Session 14's deck — say so explicitly, it's your bridge into today.**

**Q7.** Arun bought a TV with a 20% discount. Had he gotten 25% instead, he'd have saved Rs 500 more. What price did he pay?
`A` Rs 8000 · `B` Rs 12000 · `C` Rs 10000 · `D` Rs 6000
→ **A.** *Targets:* the *difference* between two discount rates on the same Marked Price is what maps to the Rs 500 gap — another Discount problem hiding in yesterday's deck.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, project the distribution after each. Total 7 min including your reads.

## Slide Block A (10–22 min) — DELIVER AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — see Instructor Notes -->
Covers: Recap (Profit & Loss) → Agenda (Discount) → the four opening worked problems: the "buy 3 get 1 free" discount%, the successive-discount-to-single-discount conversion, the successive-vs-flat shopkeeper comparison, and the combined markup/discount/profit-loss problem.

**Note (authored — the extracted deck text has no separate formula slide):** state the two core definitions explicitly before the first problem, since the deck teaches them entirely through worked examples rather than a definitions slide:
- **Discount = Marked Price − Selling Price**, and **Discount% is always calculated on Marked Price.**
- Profit/Loss%, from Session 14, is always calculated on **Cost Price**. Two different prices, two different bases — that pairing is the spine of this whole session.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **"Buy 3, get 1 free" problem:** you pay for 3 items but walk out with 4, so you effectively paid 3/4 of the marked value for each → discount = 1 − 3/4 = **25%**. Work it as a fraction, not a guess.
- **Successive discounts of 10%, 20%, 25% → single equivalent discount:** multiply the *retained* fractions — 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.54 → a single discount of **46%**, not 10+20+25=55%. Call back explicitly to Warm-Up Poll Q4 (Samsung revenue) — same multiplicative rule.
- **Two shopkeepers — successive 15% & 10% vs. flat 25%:** compute successive as 0.85 × 0.90 = 0.765 → effective discount ≈ 23.5%, which is *less* than a flat 25%. **Flat 25% is the better deal for the buyer.** This is the single most important beat in the block — dwell on it.
- **CP = X, marked 20% above CP, sold at 30% discount off MP:** MP = 1.2X; SP = 1.2X × 0.7 = 0.84X. Since SP < CP, that's a **loss of 16%**. Narrate every step as "of what?" — CP, then MP, then MP again — so students see the base switch happening.

**Checkpoint (at 22 min)** — cold-call two students:
> *"In the shopkeeper comparison, why isn't a 15% discount followed by a 10% discount the same as one flat 25% discount — and which one should you actually buy from?"*
> **Answer:** The second discount applies to the already-reduced price, not the original one, so you multiply retained fractions: 0.85 × 0.90 = 0.765, an effective 23.5% discount — less than a flat 25%. Buy from the flat-25% shop.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Think–Pair–Share: Which Deal Wins? (22–29 min)

**Format:** Think–Pair–Share · **Exposes:** the "successive discounts just add up" misconception, using the deck's own two comparison problems back to back.

**Setup line (say this):**
> *"Two problems, both from today's deck. Ninety seconds alone, working on paper. Then sixty seconds with your neighbour to agree on both answers. Then I call on pairs — you don't get to just say the number, you say the multiplication that got you there."*

**Problem 1 (individual, 45 s):** *Find the single discount equivalent to three successive discounts of 10%, 20% and 25%.*

**Problem 2 (individual, 45 s):** *One shopkeeper offers successive discounts of 15% and 10%. A second shopkeeper offers a flat 25% discount on the same item. Which shopkeeper should you buy from?*

**What students do:** Solve both alone (90 s total), then compare answers and method with a partner (60 s), then two or three pairs present their working to the class.

**Answers**

| # | Method | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.54 → discount = 1 − 0.54 | **46%** |
| 2 | Successive: 0.85 × 0.90 = 0.765 → 23.5% effective. Flat: 25%. 25% > 23.5% | **Flat 25% shop** |

**How it surfaces:** Ask the pairs who answered Problem 2 with "they're the same, 25% either way" to say their working out loud — then run the actual multiplication on the board next to it. The gap between "15+10=25" and "0.765→23.5%" is the whole point; let the room see the two numbers side by side.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Percentages don't stack by addition once they're applied one after another — they stack by multiplication. Every 'successive discount' or 'successive increase' problem you meet from here on uses exactly this move."*

**Cut rule:** If running long, drop Problem 1 and open directly with Problem 2 — it carries both the computation and the comparison, and is the one debrief line you cannot cut.

## Slide Block B (29–41 min) — DELIVER AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — see Instructor Notes -->
Covers: the deck's "Quiz Time" problem set — the chair reverse-discount problem, the tiered photocopy-discount problem, the required-markup-for-target-profit problem, and the "double discount" final-price problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Chair problem (reverse-solve a second discount):** MP = Rs 12,800, first discount 20% → Rs 10,240. Final price after a second discount = Rs 9,216. So (1 − d) = 9216 ÷ 10240 = 0.90 → second discount = **10%**. The lesson: apply the *known* discount first, then solve for the unknown one against the *reduced* price — never against the original MP.
- **Photocopy problem (tiered/partial discount):** Rs 1/sheet, 2% discount only on sheets *after* the first 1000, total 5000 sheets. First 1000 sheets @ Rs 1 = Rs 1,000. Remaining 4,000 @ Rs 0.98 = Rs 3,920. Total = **Rs 4,920.** Flag hard: the discount does **not** apply to the whole order, only the portion above the threshold.
- **Required markup for target profit despite a discount:** wants 20% profit on CP even after a 40% discount on MP. With CP = 100: need SP = 120; MP × 0.6 = 120 → MP = 200 → **CP must be marked up by 100%** to set that Marked Price. Flag the wording trap: "cost price should be increased by X%" here actually means "marked *above* cost price by X%" — the CP itself never changes.
- **"Double discount" final price:** MP = Rs 2,000, successive discounts of 20% and 15%. 2000 × 0.80 = 1,600; 1,600 × 0.85 = **Rs 1,360.** Same multiplicative rule as Activity 1, different wording ("double discount") — say this out loud, exam questions disguise the same mechanic under different names.

**Checkpoint (at 41 min)** — show hands:
> *"In the chair problem, why can't you just subtract 20% + 10% = 30% straight off Rs 12,800?"*
> **Answer:** Because the second discount is taken on the price *after* the first discount (Rs 10,240), not on the original Rs 12,800 — subtracting a combined 30% would wrongly give Rs 8,960 instead of the correct Rs 9,216.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Trace the Table: Solve the Chair Problem Backwards (41–49 min)

**Format:** Trace the Table · **Exposes:** the instinct to solve reverse-discount problems by subtracting instead of dividing out the known step.

**Setup line (say this):**
> *"We're rebuilding the chair problem on the board, one row at a time, and I want the unknown discount left as a letter until the very last row. No one guesses the answer early — we get there by the table."*

**Before class:** draw a 2-column table on the board: `Stage | Price`.

**Run it, live, filling the table with student answers at each row:**

| Stage | Price |
|---|---|
| Marked Price | Rs 12,800 |
| After first discount (20%) | 12,800 × 0.80 = **Rs 10,240** |
| After second discount (d%, unknown) | 10,240 × (1 − d) |
| Given: final selling price | **Rs 9,216** |

**Ask the class to close the table:** *"Set row 3 equal to row 4. Solve for d."*

10,240 × (1 − d) = 9,216 → (1 − d) = 0.90 → **d = 10%**

**How it surfaces:** If a student proposes "12,800 − 9,216 = Rs 3,584, so find what % that is of 12,800," let them finish it (it gives ~28%, not 10%) and then ask why it doesn't match the deck's answer. The mismatch — comparing straight to the *original* MP instead of the *intermediate* price — is more convincing than telling them the rule up front.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Every reverse discount problem is this same table: fill in what you know top-down, leave the unknown as a letter, and only compare prices that sit on the same row."*

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students want to jump straight to 10% because "it's a round number" | Make them fill every row anyway — the table, not the guess, is what's being taught. |
| Someone divides 9,216 by 12,800 directly | Ask what that ratio actually represents (the *combined* retained fraction, 0.72) and whether that answers "what was the second discount alone." |
| Room finishes early | Repeat the same table structure but swap in the "double discount" numbers from Slide Block B (MP 2,000, discounts 20% & 15%) as a forward check. |

**Cut rule:** If short on time, build only rows 1–3 as a class and solve row 4 yourself out loud, narrating each step rather than eliciting it.

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Rapid Fire Board Race (49–57 min)

**Format:** Rapid Fire Board Race · **Exposes:** whether the successive/tiered/reverse-discount skills from today hold up under time pressure, using the deck's own three remaining Quiz Time problems.

**Setup line (say this):**
> *"Three problems, one at a time, straight from today's deck. Ninety seconds each, working alone on paper. When I call time, hold your answer up — first correct answer on the board gets the point for their row. Then we run the method together before moving to the next one."*

**Before class:** split the room into rows/teams, have a tally on the board, scrap paper ready.

**Problem 1 (90 s):** *It costs Rs 1 to photocopy a sheet. A 2% discount applies to all photocopies done after the first 1,000 sheets. What does it cost to copy 5,000 sheets?*
**Answer:** 1,000 × 1 + 4,000 × 0.98 = 1,000 + 3,920 = **Rs 4,920.**

**Problem 2 (90 s):** *By what % should the cost price be marked up so that a shopkeeper earns a 20% profit even after a 40% discount on the marked price?*
**Answer:** With CP = 100, need SP = 120; MP × 0.6 = 120 → MP = 200 → markup = **100%.**

**Problem 3 (90 s):** *Marked price Rs 2,000, successive discounts of 20% and 15%. Find the final selling price.*
**Answer:** 2,000 × 0.80 × 0.85 = **Rs 1,360.**

**How it surfaces:** After each round, run the correct method on the board even if a team already shouted the right number — a right answer with a guessed method still needs the working shown for the rest of the room.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Three different disguises — a threshold, a target-profit markup, a 'double discount' — and every single one collapsed into the same two moves: split what needs splitting, then multiply retained fractions in order."*

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| A team applies the 2% discount to all 5,000 sheets in Problem 1 | Point back at "after the first 1,000" — ask them to name exactly which 4,000 sheets qualify. |
| A team is confused why Problem 2 says "cost price should be increased" | Clarify explicitly: it means the *marked* price is set above cost price — the CP itself is unchanged. |
| Everyone finishes Problem 3 instantly | Good — ask them to state it as "double discount" AND as "successive discount" and confirm both phrasings give the identical calculation. |

**Common instructor mistake:** rushing straight to the next problem after a correct shout-out. The race creates energy; the board-work afterwards is what actually teaches.

**Cut rule:** If time is short, run Problems 1 and 3 only — they cover the two skills (tiered discount, successive/double discount) most likely to reappear on an exam; drop Problem 2's markup-wording problem first.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Successive discounts add up (15% + 10% = 25%, same as a flat 25%) | Percentages "feel" additive from school arithmetic | Slide Block A's shopkeeper comparison and Activity 1, Problem 2 — compute both numbers side by side (23.5% vs 25%) |
| Discount% and Profit/Loss% share the same base | Both are "just a percentage off a price" | Narrating every calculation as "% of CP" or "% of MP" out loud in the markup/discount/loss problem |
| A bigger-sounding combined number always beats a single flat number | Numbers are compared without computing | Activity 1 — the 15%+10% vs. flat 25% result surprises most of the room the first time |
| A tiered/partial discount applies to the whole quantity | Easier to apply one rate to everything | Photocopy problem in Slide Block B — explicitly split the 1,000 no-discount sheets from the 4,000 discounted ones |
| In reverse problems, you subtract percentages instead of dividing out the known step | Subtraction feels like "undoing" a discount | Activity 2's table — comparing the wrong subtraction (~28%) against the correct answer (10%) live |
| "Cost price should be increased by X%" means CP itself changes | The sentence reads that way literally | Slide Block B / Activity 3, Problem 2 — clarify it means the Marked Price is set X% above the (unchanged) CP |
