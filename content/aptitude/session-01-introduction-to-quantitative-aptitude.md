## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Define aptitude and state how it differs from general mathematics ability. *(REMEMBERING)*
2. List at least three real-world contexts where aptitude is tested — recruitment drives, qualifying exams, government exams. *(REMEMBERING)*
3. Explain why aptitude is assessed so widely, using the deck's own "Applications" categories (financial services, technology & engineering, R&D, personal development). *(UNDERSTANDING)*
4. Describe the structure of this Aptitude course — its four components and its delivery format (sessions, skill assessments, module quizzes, practice sessions, study material). *(REMEMBERING)*
5. State what they should walk away with by the end of the course, per the deck's "Achievements" slide. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
6. Apply an assume-and-adjust reasoning strategy to solve a classic heads-and-legs riddle. *(APPLYING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Diagnostic (0–7 min)

> **This session is the exception.** There is no previous session to recall, so the poll is a *diagnostic*, not retrieval practice. No wrong answers. Purpose is to calibrate pace, gauge how much of "Extent of Aptitude" is new information versus confirmation, and establish that this classroom expects everyone to answer.

Say: *"Seven quick questions. Nobody is graded, nobody is named. I need to know who I'm teaching."*

**Q1.** Have you attempted an aptitude test before — school, coaching, mock test, recruitment, anything?
`A` Never · `B` Once or twice · `C` Several times · `D` Regularly practice
→ *Read:* If A+B > 60%, this is genuinely day one for most of the room — go slower through the "Extent of Aptitude" slides, don't assume familiarity with the company/exam names.

**Q2.** Where do you think aptitude tests show up most? *(MSQ — pick up to 2)*
`A` College placements / recruitment drives · `B` Government exam eligibility · `C` Entrance exams (CAT / GATE / GRE-type) · `D` I don't know
→ *Read:* This is a straight preview of the "Extent of Aptitude" content coming in Slide Block A — note how spread out the answers are.

**Q3.** In your own words — what's the difference between "aptitude" and "maths," if any?
`A` No difference, same thing · `B` Aptitude is maths done faster · `C` Aptitude tests reasoning and thinking, not just calculation · `D` Not sure
→ *Read:* C is where today lands. If very few pick C, that's exactly the gap this session closes — don't correct it now, let Slide Block A do it.

**Q4.** How comfortable are you solving a puzzle or riddle under a little time pressure?
`A` Very uncomfortable · `B` A bit nervous · `C` Fairly comfortable · `D` I enjoy it
→ *Read:* Calibrates the energy you bring into Activity 3 (the riddle) later in the session.

**Q5.** Which of these have you already heard of? *(MSQ — pick as many as apply)*
`A` TCS / Infosys / Wipro-type company drives · `B` CAT / GATE / GRE-type entrance exams · `C` UPSC / SSC / bank-type government exams · `D` None of these
→ *Read:* Tells you how much of the Extent-of-Aptitude lists will land as "new information" versus "confirmation of what I already knew." If D is high, slow down there.

**Q6.** What's your main reason for taking this course?
`A` Placement / job readiness · `B` Clearing a specific competitive exam · `C` General problem-solving skill · `D` Not sure yet
→ No wrong answer — pure calibration of what to lean on when you motivate later sessions.

**Q7.** How do you prefer a session like this to run?
`A` Mostly listen, take notes · `B` Discuss in pairs or groups · `C` Solve problems live, out loud · `D` A mix of all three
→ *Read:* If C/D dominate, lean harder into Activity 3 and keep the lecture stretches tight.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, project the distribution after each. Never name individuals. Total 7 min including your reads.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Real-World Callout (24–31 min)

### What this activity is

Students shout out any company, exam, or government test they personally know someone who has taken an aptitude round for — it does not have to be from the deck's lists. You capture the callouts on the board next to the real lists from Slide Block A.

### Why it's here

The poll (Q2, Q5) and the Hook already established that aptitude testing is widespread. This activity makes it personal — every student names their own real-world evidence instead of just nodding at a slide.

### Before class

Have the "Extent of Aptitude" lists (recruitment drives, qualifying tests, government exams) visible on screen or written as headers on the board, ready to add student callouts underneath.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–4:30 | Take callouts, write on board under the matching category | Shout out names |
| 4:30–6:00 | Push once on 2–3 callouts | Say what they know about that aptitude round |
| 6:00–7:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Thirty seconds — call out any company, exam, or government test you personally know someone who's taken an aptitude round for. Doesn't have to be from our list. I'll write it under the right category."*

### How it surfaces

For 2–3 of the callouts, push once: *"What do you know about that round — was it timed, was it online, did they say what it covered?"* Accept anything students actually know; you're building evidence, not testing them.

### Debrief line

> *"Notice something — every single callout you just gave me lands in one of these four categories. This isn't a syllabus topic invented for a classroom. It's a filter used everywhere, and you've already brushed up against it."*

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Room stays silent | Prime it yourself: *"I'll start — my cousin took the TCS aptitude round last year."* Then open the floor again. |
| Everyone names the same 1–2 companies | Point at the Qualifying Tests or Government Exams lists: *"What about an exam, not a company?"* |
| A callout doesn't fit any of the four categories | Take it anyway and place it loosely — the point is breadth, not taxonomy. |

**Common instructor mistake:** correcting or categorising too precisely. This activity is meant to feel loose and fast — over-managing it kills the energy the Hook just built.

**Cut rule:** If running short, cap it at 5 callouts total and skip the push-for-detail step. Do not cut the debrief line.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Think–Pair–Share: Course Self-Assessment (43–49 min)

### What this activity is

In pairs, students look at the four course components and the Achievements slide and say out loud which component they expect to find toughest, which they expect to enjoy, and which Achievement matters most to them personally.

### Why it's here

Slide Block B just listed the course's shape in the abstract. This activity makes each student locate themselves inside it before the course actually starts — a light self-assessment, not a test.

### Before class

Keep the four-component slide and the Achievements slide both visible or written on the board side by side.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:20–2:00 | Wait | Pair up, discuss |
| 2:00–5:00 | Call on 3 pairs | Share out |
| 5:00–6:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Turn to the person next to you. Of the four components on screen — Quantitative Aptitude, Logical Reasoning, Advanced Aptitude, Numerical Ability — which do you expect to be toughest for you, and which do you expect to enjoy? Ninety seconds, then I'm calling on three pairs."*

### Real content used

The four course components (slide 10) and the three Achievements (slide 12): complete understanding of aptitude concepts, methodologies & approaches to solve problems, ability to solve recruitment assessments.

### Debrief line

> *"Whatever you just said is toughest — that's fine, that's exactly what this course is for. And notice: today, and for a while, we're inside Quantitative Aptitude specifically. That's the component you just named, one way or another."*

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs go silent or say "I don't know any of these yet" | That's a valid answer — ask them to guess based on the name alone, then revisit the guess in a later session. |
| Everyone names the same component as "toughest" | Good signal — note it, and say you'll come back to it when that module starts. |
| A pair wants to skip ahead and ask what's actually *in* each component | Answer briefly but redirect: *"Fair question — that's Sessions 2 onward. Today is just the map."* |

**Common instructor mistake:** treating this as a quiz on the four names rather than a self-assessment. There is no wrong pairing — the value is students hearing themselves commit to an expectation out loud.

**Cut rule:** If running short, call on 2 pairs instead of 3 and drop the Achievements half of the question — keep only "which component do you expect to be toughest."

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Live Solve: The Goats-and-Hens Riddle (49–56 min)

### What this activity is

The riddle posed at the end of Slide Block B — 43 heads, 126 legs, some goats, some hens — gets solved live. Students commit to an answer before you reveal anything, then you walk through the deck's own built-in solving steps exactly as it presents them.

### Why it's here

This is the deck's only worked problem, and its technique (assume one case, then measure the gap) is the single transferable "quant aptitude" skill this session actually delivers. It deserves full attention and its own slot rather than being rushed as part of Slide Block B.

### Before class

Have the riddle slide ready to advance step by step: the question first, then "Assume all animals are hens," then the 86-legs count, then the reveal that some are goats.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, re-state the riddle and the four options | Listen |
| 0:30–2:30 | Wait, silent | Work alone or with a neighbour |
| 2:30–3:00 | Take a show-of-hands vote on A/B/C/D | Vote |
| 3:00–6:00 | Reveal the deck's own steps one at a time | Watch, react |
| 6:00–7:00 | Confirm the answer, debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Forty-three heads, one hundred twenty-six legs, some goats, some hens. A) 26, B) 32, C) 20, D) 23. Pick one before I show you anything. Two minutes — work it out alone or with your neighbour."*

After the vote:

> *"Now watch how the slide does it. Assume, just for a second, that every single animal is a hen."*

### The reveal (exactly as the deck presents it)

1. **Assume all 43 are hens.** Total legs would be 43 × 2 = **86**.
2. **But the question says there are 126 legs.** Why the difference?
3. **Because some of them are goats!** The gap is 126 − 86 = **40 extra legs**.
4. Each goat contributes 2 more legs than a hen would (4 − 2). So the number of goats = 40 ÷ 2 = **20**.

**Answer: C) 20 goats** (and 23 hens). Verify out loud: 20 goats × 4 legs = 80, plus 23 hens × 2 legs = 46, total = 126 legs; 20 + 23 = 43 heads. ✔

### Debrief line

> *"No algebra, no equations — just 'assume the simple case, then measure the gap.' That's the kind of technique this course is built on. You'll use some version of this trick constantly."*

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Most of the room votes B) 32 or D) 23 | Don't correct yet — run the full reveal, then ask them to re-check their pick against the verification step. |
| A student solves it with algebra (2 equations, 2 unknowns) before you reveal | Welcome it — ask them to explain their method too, then show the slide's method as a faster alternative for exactly this shape of problem. |
| Students want to know if this trick always works | Say honestly: it works for "two types, two totals" problems like this one; more general methods come in later sessions. |
| Nobody votes / room hesitates | Lower the pressure: *"No wrong guesses yet — just commit to a letter so you have skin in the game for the reveal."* |

**Common instructor mistake:** revealing the answer before the vote is locked in. The vote is what makes the reveal land — skipping it turns this into a lecture slide again.

**Cut rule:** If running short, skip the show-of-hands vote and go straight to the reveal, but keep the "assume all hens" framing intact — that framing *is* the technique being taught.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| "Aptitude is just another maths subject" | School experience only tested calculation, never framed reasoning-under-time separately | Slide Block A's explicit aptitude-vs-maths beat, reinforced by poll Q3 |
| "Aptitude only matters for IT company drives" | Recruitment drives are the most visible/talked-about category | Activity 1 and the full four-category spread in Slide Block A (recruitment, qualifying tests, government exams, applications) |
| "A riddle like this needs algebra to solve properly" | School conditioning that word problems require setting up equations | Activity 3's reveal — the deck's own assume-and-adjust technique, no equations |
| "This course is about calculating fast" | "Aptitude" sounds speed-focused | Slide Block B's Achievements beat — "methodologies & approaches," not just speed |
| "I've never really seen an aptitude question before" | Students don't recognise school/mock-test puzzles as "aptitude" until named as such | Warm-up poll Q1/Q5 usually surfaces prior exposure most students didn't label correctly |
