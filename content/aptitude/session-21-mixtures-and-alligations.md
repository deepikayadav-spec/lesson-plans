## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. State the alligation rule as a ratio of distances from the mean value: cheaper : dearer = (dearer − mean) : (mean − cheaper). *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Explain why alligation is a direct extension of the ratio/weighted-average reasoning already used in Sessions 18 and 20. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the alligation cross to find the mixing ratio of two ingredients — by price, by profit %, or by concentration — for a given target mean value. *(APPLYING)*
4. Apply weighted-average reasoning to a three-component mixture to find an unknown component's value. *(APPLYING)*
5. Convert a ratio into an actual quantity when one component's absolute amount is known, and solve withdraw-and-replace mixture problems. *(APPLYING)*
6. Analyse mixture problems that require converting cost price to selling price, or percentage to fraction, before alligation can be applied directly. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice on Session 20 (0–7 min)

7 questions, newly authored from Session 20's real company-specific questions. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals. **Q1–Q3 are pulled from Session 20's Ratios & Proportions questions on purpose** — they're today's bridge, so read them a little slower than the rest.

**Q1.** *(Ratio)* The ratio of the length to the breadth of a rectangle is 7:6. The perimeter of the plot is 260 m. Find the area of the plot.
`A` 4800 sq.m · `B` 5600 sq.m · `C` 4200 sq.m · `D` 2600 sq.m
→ **C.** *Targets:* turning a ratio into actual lengths using one more fact (the perimeter) — exactly the move you'll need today to turn an alligation ratio into an actual quantity.

**Q2.** *(Ratio)* Anil and Biswas invest Rs. 3,000 and Rs. 4,000 respectively in a business. Anil doubles his capital after 6 months. In what proportion should they divide that year's profit?
`A` 6:5 · `B` 9:8 · `C` 5:6 · `D` 8:9
→ **B.** *Targets:* a ratio that changes partway through — weighting by time, the same instinct as weighting by quantity in a mixture.

**Q3.** *(Ratio)* The salaries of Ravi and Sumit are in the ratio 2:3. If the salary of each is increased by Rs. 4,000, the new ratio becomes 35:40. What is Ravi's salary?
`A` Rs. 1,600 · `B` Rs. 2,400 · `C` Rs. 4,600 · `D` Rs. 6,400
→ **A.** *Targets:* setting up a ratio as an algebraic equation and solving for the unknown multiplier — the same skill the alligation cross needs.

**Q4.** *(SI)* In how many years will a principal of Rs. 200 generate the same interest at 6% as a principal of Rs. 800 generates in 2 years at 4.5%?
`A` 6 · `B` 7 · `C` 8 · `D` 9
→ **A.**

**Q5.** *(SI)* A sum of money at simple interest amounts to Rs. 815 in 3 years and Rs. 854 in 4 years. What was the initial sum?
`A` Rs. 698 · `B` Rs. 700 · `C` Rs. 565 · `D` Rs. 400
→ **A.**

**Q6.** *(CI)* A sum of Rs. 8,000 will amount to Rs. 8,820 in 2 years if the interest is calculated every year. The rate of compound interest is:
`A` 6% · `B` 7% · `C` 3% · `D` 5%
→ **D.**

**Q7.** *(CI, hardest — last)* The difference between the compound interest and the simple interest on a certain sum at 12% per annum for 2 years is Rs. 90. What will be the amount at the end of 3 years, compounded annually?
`A` Rs. 9,000 · `B` Rs. 6,250 · `C` Rs. 8,530.80 · `D` Rs. 8,780.80
→ **D.** *If this is weak:* don't reteach CI−SI here, just note it — today's session doesn't depend on it.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, project the distribution after each. Total 7 min including reads.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say, right after the poll closes on Q3:

> *"Look at what you just did three times in a row. Two salaries in the ratio 2:3. Two investments in the ratio 3000:4000. Two sides of a rectangle in the ratio 7:6. Every one of those was a straight ratio between two known things."*

Draw a plain number line on the board: a point for a **cheap** value, a point for a **dear** value, and a point for a **mean** value sitting between them.

> *"Today the ratio doesn't compare two given things directly — it compares how far each thing sits from an average. Milk costs Rs. 12 a litre. Water costs Rs. 0. I want a mixture worth Rs. 8 a litre. What ratio of milk to water gives me that? You don't know yet — but you already know how to think in ratios. That's all alligation is: the ratio skill from Sessions 18 and 20, pointed at distances from a mean instead of at two raw quantities."*

Point at the agenda framing from the deck itself:

> *"Concept of quantity and ratio, then mixtures, then alligations — that's today, in that order, and each one leans on the last."*

## Slide Block A (10–22 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — deck has no explicit slide-block labels; grouping follows the deck's own agenda slide (quantity/ratio → mixtures → alligations) and the run of examples before the first "Quiz Time" divider -->
Covers: Concept of quantity and ratio → Mixtures & Alligations → the Alligation Cross → the pulses-mixing example → the water-and-milk example.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **The alligation cross is a ratio, not a new topic.** cheaper : dearer = (dearer price − mean price) : (mean price − cheaper price). Write it once, leave it on the board for the whole session.
- **Worked example 1 (pulses):** mix pulses at Rs. 15/kg and Rs. 20/kg to get a mixture worth Rs. 16.50/kg. Cross gives (20 − 16.50) : (16.50 − 15) = 3.5 : 1.5 = **7 : 3**. Deck's own hint: *"Use Alligation methodology to solve."*
- **Worked example 2 (water + milk):** water is a real ingredient worth **Rs. 0/litre**. Milk at Rs. 12/litre, target mixture Rs. 8/litre. Cross gives (12 − 8) : (8 − 0) = 4 : 8 = **1 : 2** (water : milk). Deck's own hint: *"Use Alligation rule & Remember water costs Rs 0."* Flag this explicitly — "free" or "zero-value" components are the first place students go wrong.

**Checkpoint (at 22 min)** — cold-call two students:
> *"State the alligation rule in one sentence. Then use it: pulses at Rs. 15/kg and Rs. 20/kg, mixture worth Rs. 16.50/kg — what ratio?"*
> **Answer:** Rule — the ratio of quantities equals the ratio of the distances of the *other* price from the mean, cross-wise. Ratio: **7 : 3**.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Trace the Table (22–29 min)

### What this activity is

Three worked problems from the deck's own example run, solved on the board as a shared table (Item | Price or % | Ratio-part | Weighted contribution), built up row by row with the class rather than delivered as a finished slide.

### Why it's here

Block A only ever mixed **two** things by **price**. This activity stretches the same one rule across three fresh situations — profit percentage instead of price, three ingredients instead of two, and an unknown *ingredient value* instead of an unknown *ratio* — before students see it done any other way.

### Before class

Have the three problems ready to write up; leave three empty tables on the board.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, put Problem 1 on the board | Listen |
| 0:30–2:30 | Build the table for Problem 1 with the class calling out each cell | Call out values |
| 2:30–4:30 | Problem 2, same treatment | Call out values |
| 4:30–6:30 | Problem 3, same treatment | Call out values |
| 6:30–7:00 | Debrief line | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Same rule, three different disguises. I'm not solving these for you — I'm writing what you tell me. If the table doesn't balance, we'll see it before we finish."*

### The problems

**Problem 1 (profit %, not price):** A shopkeeper has 1000 kg of sugar. Part is sold at 8% profit, the rest at 18% profit. Overall gain is 14%. How much was sold at 18% profit?
Alligation on the *profit percentages*: 8% : 18% around a mean of 14% → (18−14):(14−8) = 4:6 = **2:3**. The "3" part is the 18%-profit share of 1000 kg → **600 kg**.

**Problem 2 (three components):** Tea worth Rs. 126/kg and Rs. 135/kg is mixed with a third variety in the ratio 1:1:2. The mixture is worth Rs. 153/kg. Find the price of the third variety.
Weighted mean: (1×126 + 1×135 + 2×x) / 4 = 153 → 126 + 135 + 2x = 612 → 2x = 351 → **x = Rs. 175.5/kg**.

**Problem 3 (unknown second rate):** Rice at Rs. 9/kg is mixed with another quality in the ratio 1:3. The mixture is worth Rs. 12/kg. Find the rate of the second quality.
Weighted mean: (1×9 + 3×x) / 4 = 12 → 9 + 3x = 48 → 3x = 39 → **x = Rs. 13/kg**.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students try to force Problem 1 into a "price" cross | Say plainly: alligation works on *any* quantity you're averaging — price, profit %, speed, whatever. Only the labels change. |
| Nobody sees why Problem 2 needs 4 parts, not 2 | Point at the ratio 1:1:2 — that's 4 total parts, so the weighted sum divides by 4, not 2. |
| A student solves Problem 3 with the alligation cross instead of the weighted-mean equation | Good — show both side by side; they give the same answer because the cross *is* the weighted-mean equation rearranged. |
| Room is slow to call out values | You fill the first row yourself as a model, then hand the marker back. |

**Common instructor mistake:** solving these silently and just writing the final answer. The table only teaches anything if the class watches each cell get filled and can spot an error before the total.

**Cut rule:** If short on time, run Problem 1 and Problem 3 only — Problem 2 (three-component) can be folded into Slide Block B's opening line as a "you already did this in Activity 1" callback instead.

## Slide Block B (29–40 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — the deck's first "Quiz Time" divider slide has no content of its own; the two problems immediately after it (cost-price→selling-price alligation, and solving for an actual quantity) read as a direct continuation of the same worked-example format, so they are treated as slide content rather than as a separate quiz -->
Covers: mixing at a target *selling* price when a profit % is involved → solving for an actual quantity (not just a ratio) when one component's amount is already known.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **When the target is a selling price with a profit %, alligation still runs on cost price — you have to convert first.** Sugar varieties at Rs. 30/kg and Rs. 40/kg are sold as a mixture at Rs. 36/kg for a 10% gain. The mean *selling* price of Rs. 36 corresponds to a mean *cost* price of 36 ÷ 1.10 = Rs. 32.7272.../kg. Alligation on cost prices: (40 − 32.73) : (32.73 − 30) ≈ 7.27 : 2.73 = **8 : 3**. Deck's own hint: *"Convert cost price of two varieties into selling price."* Say it out loud as: *"Alligation always compares like with like — cost with cost, or selling price with selling price. Never mix the two."*
- **A ratio becomes a quantity the moment you're told one real amount.** How many kg of rice at Rs. 20/kg must mix with 30 kg of rice at Rs. 30/kg to get a Rs. 26/kg mixture? Cross: (30−26):(26−20) = 4:6 = 2:3. The "3" part of the ratio *is* the known 30 kg, so 1 part = 10 kg, and the "2" part (the unknown rice) = **20 kg**. This is the first time in the session a ratio turns into a real number — flag it as a distinct extra step, not something the cross gives you for free.

**Checkpoint (at 40 min)** — cold-call:
> *"In the rice problem, why did we get a number of kilograms instead of just a ratio?"*
> **Answer:** Because we were told one component's actual amount (30 kg), which lets us pin down what one "part" of the 2:3 ratio is worth in real kilograms — here, 1 part = 10 kg, so the unknown rice is 20 kg.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Rapid Fire Board Race (40–47 min)

### What this activity is

Two teams race to solve the deck's own MCQ-style problems on the board. First team to show correct working — not just a shouted letter — scores the point.

### Why it's here

Both problems are MCQs in the deck with real distractor options, which is exactly the format a board race rewards: fast, checkable working against a fixed answer key, with wrong-answer traps built in by the deck itself.

### Before class

Split the room into two halves. Have both problems ready to reveal one at a time, options included.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal Problem 1 with all four options | Listen |
| 0:30–3:00 | Time the race, first team to the board with full working | Race, solve |
| 3:00–3:30 | Confirm answer, run the numbers aloud | Watch |
| 3:30–6:00 | Repeat for Problem 2 | Race, solve |
| 6:00–7:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Two teams, two problems, real exam options including the traps. First person at the board with working I can follow — not just an answer — wins the point for their team."*

### The problems

**Problem 1:** In a can, milk and water are in the ratio 4:5. If it is filled with an additional 8 litres of milk, the can would be full, and the ratio of milk to water would become 6:5. Find the capacity of the can (in litres).
`A` 40 · `B` 44 · `C` 48 · `D` 52
Let initial milk = 4x, water = 5x. After +8 L milk: (4x+8):5x = 6:5 → 20x + 40 = 30x → x = 4. Initial milk = 16, water = 20 (total 36); can is full after adding 8 L → **capacity = 44 litres (B)**.

**Problem 2:** In a mixture of milk and water in the ratio 9:5, how much of the mixture should be withdrawn and replaced with water so the resulting mixture has equal amounts of milk and water?
`A` 3/9 · `B` 2/9 · `C` 5/9 · `D` 1/9
Milk fraction currently 9/14. Withdraw fraction x of the mixture and replace with pure water: new milk fraction = (9/14)(1−x). Want this equal to 1/2: 1−x = 7/9 → **x = 2/9 (B)**.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| A team shouts the letter without working | Doesn't count. Say it again before starting: working only. |
| Everyone picks a distractor on Problem 1 (e.g. 40 or 48) | Common trap: they solve for the ratio *before* adding milk, or forget water stays fixed at 5x. Re-run it live, pointing at which quantity actually changes. |
| Problem 2 stalls the room | Ask: *"Out of the whole mixture, what fraction is milk right now?"* — 9/14 is the fact everything else hangs off. |
| One team finishes both early | Have them explain problem 2's logic to the room while the other team finishes — keeps them engaged instead of idle. |

**Common instructor mistake:** revealing the correct letter before the working is checked. Half the value here is in catching *which* wrong option a mistake leads to.

**Cut rule:** Run Problem 1 only, and spend the freed 3 minutes narrating Problem 2's setup verbally instead of racing it.

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Think–Pair–Share (47–54 min)

### What this activity is

Students get 90 seconds alone to attempt the deck's alcohol-vessels problem, then 90 seconds to compare their working with a partner, then the room shares out.

### Why it's here

This problem needs an extra conversion step — percentage to fraction — layered on top of the mixing itself. It's the most analytical problem in the deck, so it earns a slower, paired format rather than a race.

### Before class

Have the problem and its four options ready to reveal, with the hint held back until the pair phase.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal problem, withhold hint | Listen |
| 0:30–2:00 | Silent, individual attempt | Work alone |
| 2:00–3:30 | Reveal hint, pairs compare and reconcile | Discuss in pairs |
| 3:30–6:00 | Take the answer from 2–3 pairs, run the numbers | Share, watch |
| 6:00–7:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Ninety seconds alone — no talking. Then ninety seconds with the person next to you to compare notes. I want to hear where you disagreed, not just the final answer."*

### The problem

Vessels P and Q contain 62.5% and 87.5% alcohol. 2 litres from P are mixed with 4 litres from Q. Find the ratio of alcohol to water in the resulting mixture.
`A` 16:5 · `B` 14:5 · `C` 16:7 · `D` 19:5
Deck's own hint (reveal at the 2:00 mark): *"Use percentages concept (decimal to fraction conversion)."*

Alcohol from P = 2 × 0.625 = 1.25 L. Alcohol from Q = 4 × 0.875 = 3.5 L. Total alcohol = 4.75 L. Total volume = 6 L, so water = 1.25 L. Ratio alcohol : water = 4.75 : 1.25 = **19 : 5 (D)**.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students try to alligation-cross the two percentages directly | Stop them — this problem isn't asking for a *mixing ratio* (that's fixed at 2:4 already); it's asking for the alcohol:water ratio *after* mixing. Different question. |
| Pairs disagree on 62.5% as a fraction | Ask both partners to write 62.5% as a decimal, then multiply by the litres — skip the fraction conversion entirely if it's causing friction. |
| A pair lands on 16:7 (a real distractor) | They likely added the two percentages instead of weighting by volume. Show 2 L and 4 L are not equal weights. |
| Time is short | Skip the individual 90 seconds; go straight to pairs. |

**Common instructor mistake:** solving this one for the class immediately because it looks slow. The pair-comparison step is where the percentage-to-decimal error actually gets caught — don't skip to the answer.

**Cut rule:** Drop the individual silent phase and the 2–3 pair share-outs to one; keep the hint reveal and the final run-through, since those two are the substance.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (54–60 min)

**Exit ticket** — on paper or in chat before anyone leaves:

> Water (Rs. 0/litre) is mixed with milk costing Rs. 12/litre to obtain a mixture worth Rs. 8/litre. Draw the alligation cross and give the ratio of water to milk.
> **Answer:** (12 − 8) : (8 − 0) = 4 : 8 = **1 : 2** (water : milk).

Scan responses on the way out. If the ratio comes out inverted (2:1), that's the cheaper/dearer side mix-up — open Session 22 with a 60-second re-run of the cross.

**Homework**

| Task | Instruction |
|---|---|
| Re-derive, don't re-read | Redo the sugar CP→SP problem (Rs. 30/Rs. 40/kg → Rs. 36/kg at 10% gain) and the rice-quantity problem (Rs. 20/kg + 30 kg of Rs. 30/kg → Rs. 26/kg) from a blank page, showing the full alligation cross for each. |
| Cover the options, then check | For the three MCQ problems used in Activities 2 and 3 (can capacity, withdraw-and-replace, alcohol vessels), re-solve each without looking at the options first, then check against the option you'd have picked cold. |
| Write one of your own | Write one original alligation problem — any theme (price, profit %, concentration) — with a mean value and two components, and solve it. Bring it to Session 22. |

> *"There's no practice set attached to this topic yet, so tonight's homework is the deck itself, worked cold. If you can redo every problem from today without the hint text, you've got alligation."*

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Alligation only works on prices | Every first example in the deck is a price example | Activity 1, Problem 1 — running the same cross on a profit percentage instead of a price |
| A "free" ingredient (water) doesn't need a value in the cross | It costs nothing, so it feels like it's outside the calculation | Slide Block A's explicit flag: "water costs Rs. 0" — it's a real point on the number line, not an exemption |
| The alligation cross alone gives an actual quantity | The ratio and a real number both come out as plain digits | Slide Block B's rice problem — showing the ratio (2:3) is one step, and converting it to kilograms using the known 30 kg is a second, separate step |
| You can alligate a cost price directly against a selling price | Both are "just prices" | Slide Block B's sugar problem — the deck's own hint to convert onto one basis before crossing |
| Cheaper : dearer and dearer : cheaper are interchangeable | The two numbers in the ratio look symmetric | The Exit Ticket — flagging that an inverted ratio (2:1 instead of 1:2) is the single most common wrong answer on this topic |
| Percentage alcohol strengths can be mixed like whole-number ratios | 62.5% and 87.5% look like awkward decimals to skip past | Activity 3 — converting to decimals and weighting by actual litres before combining |
