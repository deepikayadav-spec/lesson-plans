## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. State the alligation rule as a ratio of distances from the mean value. *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Explain why alligation is a direct extension of the ratio/weighted-average reasoning already used in Sessions 18 and 20. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the alligation cross to find the mixing ratio of two ingredients — by price, by profit %, or by concentration — for a given target mean value. *(APPLYING)*
4. Apply weighted-average reasoning to a three-component mixture to find an unknown component's value. *(APPLYING)*
5. Convert a ratio into an actual quantity when one component's absolute amount is known, and solve withdraw-and-replace mixture problems. *(APPLYING)*
6. Analyse mixture problems that require converting cost price to selling price, or percentage to fraction, before alligation can be applied directly. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice on Session 20 (3–7 min) · ALS: Polling

5 questions, newly authored from Session 20's real company-specific questions. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** *(Ratio)* The ratio of the length to the breadth of a rectangle is 7:6. The perimeter of the plot is 260 m. Find the area of the plot.
`A` 4800 sq.m · `B` 5600 sq.m · `C` 4200 sq.m · `D` 2600 sq.m
→ **C.** *Targets:* turning a ratio into actual lengths using one more fact — exactly the move you'll need today to turn an alligation ratio into an actual quantity.

**Q2.** *(Ratio)* Anil and Biswas invest Rs. 3,000 and Rs. 4,000 respectively in a business. Anil doubles his capital after 6 months. In what proportion should they divide that year's profit?
`A` 6:5 · `B` 9:8 · `C` 5:6 · `D` 8:9
→ **B.** *Targets:* a ratio that changes partway through — the same instinct as weighting by quantity in a mixture.

**Q3.** *(Ratio)* The salaries of Ravi and Sumit are in the ratio 2:3. If the salary of each is increased by Rs. 4,000, the new ratio becomes 35:40. What is Ravi's salary?
`A` Rs. 1,600 · `B` Rs. 2,400 · `C` Rs. 4,600 · `D` Rs. 6,400
→ **A.** *Targets:* setting up a ratio as an algebraic equation — the same skill the alligation cross needs.

**Q4.** *(SI)* In how many years will a principal of Rs. 200 generate the same interest at 6% as a principal of Rs. 800 generates in 2 years at 4.5%?
`A` 6 · `B` 7 · `C` 8 · `D` 9
→ **A.**

**Q5.** *(CI)* A sum of Rs. 8,000 will amount to Rs. 8,820 in 2 years if the interest is calculated every year. The rate of compound interest is:
`A` 6% · `B` 7% · `C` 3% · `D` 5%
→ **D.**

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question. Total ~3.75 min for the 5 questions.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say, right after the poll closes on Q3:

> *"Look at what you just did three times in a row. Two salaries in the ratio 2:3. Two investments in the ratio 3000:4000. Two sides of a rectangle in the ratio 7:6. Every one of those was a straight ratio between two known things."*

Draw a plain number line on the board: a point for a **cheap** value, a point for a **dear** value, and a point for a **mean** value sitting between them.

> *"Today the ratio doesn't compare two given things directly — it compares how far each thing sits from an average. Milk costs Rs. 12 a litre. Water costs Rs. 0. I want a mixture worth Rs. 8 a litre. What ratio of milk to water gives me that? You don't know yet — but you already know how to think in ratios. That's all alligation is: the ratio skill from Sessions 18 and 20, pointed at distances from a mean instead of at two raw quantities."*

Point at the agenda framing from the deck itself:

> *"Concept of quantity and ratio, then mixtures, then alligations — that's today, in that order, and each one leans on the last."*

## Slide Block A (10–19 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — deck has no explicit slide-block labels; grouping follows the deck's own agenda slide and the run of examples before the first "Quiz Time" divider -->
Covers: Concept of quantity and ratio → Mixtures & Alligations → the Alligation Cross → the pulses-mixing example → the water-and-milk example.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **The alligation cross is a ratio, not a new topic.** cheaper : dearer = (dearer price − mean price) : (mean price − cheaper price). Write it once, leave it on the board.
- **Worked example 1 (pulses):** mix pulses at Rs. 15/kg and Rs. 20/kg to get a mixture worth Rs. 16.50/kg. Cross gives (20−16.50):(16.50−15) = **7:3**.
- **Worked example 2 (water + milk):** water is a real ingredient worth **Rs. 0/litre**. Milk at Rs. 12/litre, target mixture Rs. 8/litre. Cross gives (12−8):(8−0) = **1:2** (water:milk). Flag this explicitly — "free" or "zero-value" components are the first place students go wrong.

**Checkpoint (at 19 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"State the alligation rule in one sentence. Then use it: pulses at Rs. 15/kg and Rs. 20/kg, mixture worth Rs. 16.50/kg — what ratio?"*
> **Answer:** Rule — the ratio of quantities equals the ratio of the distances of the *other* price from the mean, cross-wise. Ratio: **7:3**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Trace the Table (19–26 min)

**ALS format:** Guided Table Build — three worked problems solved on the board as a shared table, built up row by row with the class. Chosen right after Slide Block A because it only ever mixed **two** things by **price** — this stretches the same one rule across three fresh situations (profit percentage, three ingredients, an unknown ingredient value) before students see it done any other way.

**Setup line:**
> *"Same rule, three different disguises. I'm not solving these for you — I'm writing what you tell me. If the table doesn't balance, we'll see it before we finish."*

**Problem 1 (profit %, not price):** A shopkeeper has 1000 kg of sugar. Part is sold at 8% profit, the rest at 18% profit. Overall gain is 14%. How much was sold at 18% profit?
Alligation on the *profit percentages*: (18−14):(14−8) = **2:3**. The "3" part is the 18%-profit share of 1000 kg → **600 kg**.

**Problem 2 (three components):** Tea worth Rs. 126/kg and Rs. 135/kg is mixed with a third variety in the ratio 1:1:2. The mixture is worth Rs. 153/kg. Find the price of the third variety.
Weighted mean: (126+135+2x)/4 = 153 → **x = Rs. 175.5/kg**.

**Problem 3 (unknown second rate):** Rice at Rs. 9/kg is mixed with another quality in the ratio 1:3. The mixture is worth Rs. 12/kg. Find the rate of the second quality.
Weighted mean: (9+3x)/4 = 12 → **x = Rs. 13/kg**.

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students try to force Problem 1 into a "price" cross | Say plainly: alligation works on *any* quantity you're averaging — only the labels change. |
| Nobody sees why Problem 2 needs 4 parts, not 2 | Point at the ratio 1:1:2 — that's 4 total parts. |
| A student solves Problem 3 with the cross instead of the weighted-mean equation | Good — show both side by side; the cross *is* the weighted-mean equation rearranged. |

**Debrief line:**
> *"Three disguises, one rule. The moment you see 'a mean value sitting between two others,' the cross applies — whatever the units."*

**Cut rule:** Run Problem 1 and Problem 3 only — Problem 2 (three-component) can be folded into Slide Block B's opening line as a callback instead.

## Slide Block B (26–37 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — the deck's first "Quiz Time" divider slide has no content of its own; the two problems immediately after it read as a direct continuation of the same worked-example format -->
Covers: mixing at a target *selling* price when a profit % is involved → solving for an actual quantity when one component's amount is already known.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **When the target is a selling price with a profit %, alligation still runs on cost price — you have to convert first.** Sugar varieties at Rs. 30/kg and Rs. 40/kg are sold as a mixture at Rs. 36/kg for a 10% gain. Mean cost price = 36÷1.10 = Rs. 32.73/kg. Alligation on cost prices: **8:3**. *"Alligation always compares like with like — cost with cost, or selling price with selling price. Never mix the two."*
- **A ratio becomes a quantity the moment you're told one real amount.** How many kg of rice at Rs. 20/kg must mix with 30 kg of rice at Rs. 30/kg to get a Rs. 26/kg mixture? Cross: (30−26):(26−20) = 2:3. The "3" part of the ratio *is* the known 30 kg, so 1 part = 10 kg, unknown rice = **20 kg**. This is the first time a ratio turns into a real number — flag it as a distinct extra step.

**Trap-drill beat (2 min, compressed from a full activity):** *"In a can, milk:water = 4:5. Add 8 litres of milk, ratio becomes 6:5. Find the can's capacity."* 10 s silent, reveal: milk=4x, water=5x; (4x+8):5x=6:5 → x=4 → capacity=**44 litres**. *"The trap is forgetting water stays fixed at 5x while only milk changes — same 'what's fixed, what's changing' question as every mixture problem today."* (Compressed to protect the schedule; the withdraw-and-replace extension is available as an optional buffer closer.)

**Checkpoint (at 37 min)** — cold-call:
> *"In the rice problem, why did we get a number of kilograms instead of just a ratio?"*
> **Answer:** Because we were told one component's actual amount (30 kg), pinning down what one "part" of the 2:3 ratio is worth.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Think-Pair-Share (37–45 min)

**ALS format:** Think-Pair-Share. Chosen as the closing activity because this problem needs an extra conversion step — percentage to fraction — layered on top of the mixing itself. It's the most analytical problem in the deck, so it earns a slower, paired format rather than a race.

**Setup line:**
> *"Ninety seconds alone — no talking. Then ninety seconds with the person next to you to compare notes. I want to hear where you disagreed, not just the final answer."*

**The problem:** Vessels P and Q contain 62.5% and 87.5% alcohol. 2 litres from P are mixed with 4 litres from Q. Find the ratio of alcohol to water in the resulting mixture.
`A` 16:5 · `B` 14:5 · `C` 16:7 · `D` 19:5
Deck's own hint (reveal after the silent phase): *"Use percentages concept (decimal to fraction conversion)."*

Alcohol from P = 2×0.625=1.25L. Alcohol from Q = 4×0.875=3.5L. Total alcohol=4.75L, total volume=6L, water=1.25L. Ratio = **19:5 (D)**.

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students try to alligation-cross the two percentages directly | Stop them — this isn't asking for a *mixing ratio* (that's fixed at 2:4 already); it's asking for the alcohol:water ratio *after* mixing. |
| A pair lands on 16:7 (a real distractor) | They likely added the two percentages instead of weighting by volume. |

**Debrief line:**
> *"The pair-comparison step is where the percentage-to-decimal error actually gets caught — that's why this one is slow, not raced."*

**Cut rule:** Drop the individual silent phase, go straight to pairs; keep the hint reveal and final run-through.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (45–48 min)

**Exit ticket** (~1 min) — before anyone leaves:

> Water (Rs. 0/litre) is mixed with milk costing Rs. 12/litre to obtain a mixture worth Rs. 8/litre. Draw the alligation cross and give the ratio of water to milk.
> **Answer:** (12−8):(8−0) = **1:2** (water:milk).

Scan responses on the way out. If the ratio comes out inverted (2:1), that's the cheaper/dearer side mix-up — open Session 22 with a 60-second re-run of the cross.

**Homework**

| Task | Instruction |
|---|---|
| Re-derive, don't re-read | Redo the sugar CP→SP problem and the rice-quantity problem from a blank page, showing the full alligation cross for each. |
| Cover the options, then check | For the can-capacity and alcohol-vessels problems, re-solve without looking at the options first. |
| Write one of your own | Write one original alligation problem with a mean value and two components, and solve it. Bring it to Session 22. |

> *"There's no practice set attached to this topic yet, so tonight's homework is the deck itself, worked cold."*

## Buffer (48–50 min) · Flex — not instructional

Unscheduled on purpose. If you land here with time on the clock, run the optional withdraw-and-replace extension: *"Milk:water = 9:5. How much of the mixture should be withdrawn and replaced with water so the result is equal amounts of milk and water?"* Milk fraction = 9/14; withdraw fraction x, replace with water: (9/14)(1−x)=1/2 → **x=2/9**. Never required.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Alligation only works on prices | Every first example in the deck is a price example | ALS Activity 1, Problem 1 — running the cross on a profit percentage |
| A "free" ingredient (water) doesn't need a value in the cross | It costs nothing, so it feels outside the calculation | Slide Block A's explicit flag: water is a real point on the number line |
| The alligation cross alone gives an actual quantity | The ratio and a real number both come out as plain digits | Slide Block B's rice problem — the ratio is one step, converting to kilograms is a separate second step |
| You can alligate a cost price directly against a selling price | Both are "just prices" | Slide Block B's sugar problem — convert onto one basis before crossing |
| Cheaper : dearer and dearer : cheaper are interchangeable | The two numbers in the ratio look symmetric | The Exit Ticket — an inverted ratio is the single most common wrong answer |
| Percentage alcohol strengths can be mixed like whole-number ratios | 62.5% and 87.5% look like awkward decimals to skip past | ALS Activity 2 — converting to decimals and weighting by actual litres |
