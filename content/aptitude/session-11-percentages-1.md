## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. State the percentage-change formula and identify the two successive-change scenarios covered today (salary hikes, price–consumption, comparative "more than / less than" statements). *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Explain why a percentage increase and the percentage decrease that reverses it are **not** equal in magnitude (e.g., 40→60 kmph is a 50% rise, but 60→40 kmph back is only a 33.33% fall). *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the shortcut formula for two successive percentage changes, Net% = a + b + (ab/100), to compute the combined effect of two hikes/cuts in one step. *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve multi-step percentage-change word problems (fractions, price–expenditure, water content in fresh vs. dried grapes) by identifying which quantity stays fixed. *(APPLYING)*
5. Analyze "A is x% more than B" statements to correctly derive "B is less than A by [x/(100+x)]×100 %" instead of assuming the reverse percentage is the same number. *(ANALYZING)*
6. Evaluate ratio-based multi-person word problems (e.g., three people's money linked by percentage and multiple relationships) by structuring unknowns in a table before solving. *(EVALUATING)*

`A` 0.82 · `B` 8.2 · `C` 41 · `D` 82

`A` 0.082 · `B` 0.82 · `C` 8.2 · `D` 1.82

`A` 41.82 · `B` 51.82 · `C` 40.18 · `D` 42.64

→ *If >40% wrong:* they're not adding the blocks together. Re-run 50% + 1% = 41 + 0.82 live before moving on.

**Q4.** From the fraction–percentage table, 1/4 equals which percentage?

**Q5.** Which fraction equals 62.5%?
`A` 5/6 · `B` 3/8 · `C` 5/8 · `D` 5/9
→ **C.** *Targets:* table recall (eighths row). Distractor B (3/8 = 37.5%) is the classic "wrong numerator" mix-up.

**Q6.** Which of these fraction–percentage pairs from the table are correct? *(MSQ — select all)*

`A` 40 · `B` 60 · `C` 80 · `D` 100

## Hook (7–10 min)

Write this on the board, nothing else:

```
40 kmph  →  60 kmph
```

Say: *"A car speeds up from 40 to 60 kmph. That's a 50% increase — everyone agree?"* (They will.)

Now add:

```
60 kmph  →  40 kmph
```

Say: *"Same car, slows back down to exactly 40 kmph. What percentage decrease was that? Shout a number."*

Let the guesses land — most of the room will say "50%." Reveal:

> *"It's 33.33%. Same two speeds, same road, and the increase and the decrease are different numbers. Nobody's arithmetic is wrong — the rule that explains this is what the next thirteen minutes are for."*

Tie back to the poll: *"You just spent seven minutes doing 51% of 82 in building blocks. Today those same building blocks explain why 40-to-60 and 60-to-40 don't cancel out."*

## Slide Block A (10–23 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

Covers (slides 4–7): Percentage Change → Successive increment/decrement (conventional vs. shortcut) → the 40/60/40 kmph illustration → Sai's salary worked example.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Percentage change formula:** %Change = ((New − Old) / Old) × 100. State it once, plainly, before touching successive changes.
- **The successive-change rule from the deck's own speaker notes:** a (1/n) increase requires a (1/n+1) decrease to undo it, and a (1/n) decrease requires a (1/n−1) increase to undo it. <!-- placement: inferred — only the notes fragment was extracted; confirm the full slide wording matches this statement of the rule. --> Walk the class back through the hook's 40→60 (a 1/2 increase, n=2) and 60→40 (a 1/3 decrease, n+1=3) to show the rule isn't abstract — it's the number they just watched.
- **Sai's example (slide 7):** initial salary Rs. 20000, 10% annual hike for two years. Show **both** columns as the deck does — conventional (20000 → 22000 → 24200, year by year) and shortcut (10 + 10 + (10×10/100) = 21%). Land the point hard: *"Ten percent plus ten percent is not twenty percent. It's twenty-one, because the second hike lands on a bigger number."* Final answer: **Rs. 24200, a 21% change.**

**Checkpoint (at 23 min)** — cold-call:
> *"Using a + b + ab/100 — why isn't two 10% hikes just 20%?"*
> **Answer:** The second 10% is calculated on the already-increased salary, not the original — that extra cross-term, ab/100 = 1%, is what pushes it to 21%.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Think–Pair–Share: Lohitha's Salary (23–30 min)

### What this activity is

Students apply the shortcut formula themselves — with a mixed-sign case (one increase, one decrease) — before you reveal the deck's answer. Sai's example was watched; this one is attempted.

### Why it's here

The formula only sticks if students use it once under their own effort, with a negative term in play, before the class moves to problems where they'll need it unsupervised.

### Before class

Have Lohitha's problem ready but not yet shown in full; keep a "Conventional" / "Shortcut" two-column layout ready on the board (mirrors the deck's own layout).

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal the problem | Read |
| 0:30–2:30 | Wait, silent | Work out an answer alone using the shortcut formula |
| 2:30–4:30 | "Pair up, compare, agree on one number" | Discuss with neighbour |
| 4:30–6:00 | Cold-call 2 pairs; verify with the conventional method live | Answer, watch |
| 6:00–7:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Lohitha's salary is 30000. Year one: 20% hike. Year two: 10% decrement. Using the formula from Sai's example, work out the overall percentage change on your own — sixty seconds, no talking."*

### The problem

> Lohitha got selected in a company with an initial salary of 30000. In the first year she got a hike of 20%, second year she got a decrement of 10%. After two years what is the percentage change in her salary?

### Answer

**8% increase.**

Shortcut: 20 + (−10) + (20 × −10 / 100) = 20 − 10 − 2 = **8**.
Conventional check (verify live if a pair asks): 30000 → 36000 → 32400, which is 2400 more than 30000 → 8%.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| They answer 10% (just 20 − 10) | They dropped the cross-term entirely — the exact trap Sai's example warned about. Re-run 10+10=21 from Slide Block A as a reminder that the cross-term never disappears. |
| They answer −8% (sign flip) | Sign confusion on the decrement. Recompute term by term on the board: 20, then −10, then the product term's sign. |
| Pairs disagree on whether decrement is negative | Standardise immediately: increase = positive, decrement = negative, and the product follows normal multiplication sign rules. |

**Common instructor mistake:** giving the shortcut answer without also showing the year-by-year conventional computation. Students trust the shortcut only once they've seen it match the long way, live, at least twice.

**Cut rule:** if short on time, skip the pair-compare step — go straight from individual thinking to cold-calling two students.

## Slide Block B (30–41 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

Covers (slides 9–11): the fraction problem → the dal price/consumption problem → the Pavan/Prudhvi salary-comparison problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Fraction problem (slide 9):** numerator +40%, denominator −20%, resulting fraction 21/16 — find the original. Show the factor method: new fraction = (1.4/0.8) × original = 1.75 × original = 21/16, so original = (21/16) ÷ 1.75 = **3/4**. This is the same "multiply by a factor, don't add percentages" logic as Slide Block A.
- **Dal problem (slide 10):** price rises 10%; by how much must consumption fall to keep expenditure unchanged? Deck's answer: **9 1/11%**, not 10%. Formula: %decrease = (x / (100 + x)) × 100 = (10/110) × 100 = 9 1/11%. Say explicitly: *"The decrease needed is always smaller than the increase that caused it — because it's calculated on the new, bigger price."*
- **Pavan/Prudhvi problem (slide 11):** Pavan's salary is 40% more than Prudhvi's. By what % is Prudhvi's less than Pavan's? Deck's answer: **28.5714%**, not 40%. The two percentages are measured against **different bases** — "40% more" is relative to Prudhvi's salary; "% less" is relative to Pavan's salary.

**Checkpoint (at 41 min)** — cold-call:
> *"If A is 40% more than B, is B automatically 40% less than A? What's the real number, and why?"*
> **Answer:** No — 28.5714% less. The base is different each way: 40% more is measured on B, but the "less than" figure is measured on A.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Rapid Fire Board Race (41–48 min)

### What this activity is

Two "Quiz Time" problems from the deck, raced in teams against the clock — each requires chaining a percentage-of-a-remaining-amount, not a single-step calculation.

### Why it's here

Straight after Slide Block B's near-miss trap (40% ≠ 28.5714%), this activity forces the same "don't just subtract/add percentages of different bases" discipline under time pressure, on two fresh scenarios.

### Before class

Split the class into 2–3 teams/rows. Have both problems ready to reveal one at a time. Stopwatch or phone timer visible.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–3:00 | Reveal Problem 1 (vendor oranges), start clock | Race in teams, full working required |
| 3:00–3:30 | Take the first correct full working, verify live | Watch, self-check |
| 3:30–6:00 | Reveal Problem 2 (dried grapes), start clock | Race again |
| 6:00–7:00 | Verify + debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Two problems, teams racing. First team with the correct answer AND the full working on the board scores the point — a shouted number with no working doesn't count."*

### Problem 1

> A vendor sells 70% of the oranges he had and throws away 20% of the remaining oranges. The next day, he sells 50% of the remaining oranges and throws away the rest. What % of the oranges does the vendor throw away?

**Answer: 18%.**
Working: sells 70%, 30% remains. Throws away 20% of that 30% = 6% (day 1); 24% remains. Day 2: sells 50% of the 24% = 12%, throws away the rest = 12%. Total thrown = 6 + 12 = **18%**.

### Problem 2

> Fresh grapes contain 80% water by weight while dried grapes contain 40% water by weight. What is the weight of dry grapes available from 20 kg of fresh grapes?

**Answer: 6.66 Kg.**
Working: solid matter in fresh grapes = 20% of 20 kg = 4 kg, and this doesn't change on drying. In dried grapes, solids = 60% of dried weight → dried weight = 4 ÷ 0.6 = **6.66 kg**.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Teams add 20% + 50% = 70% thrown (oranges) | Each percentage is of a *different* remaining amount, not the original total. Rebuild the 30% → 24% chain on the board. |
| Teams forget day 2's "throws away the rest" is only 12%, not another 50% | Point out "the rest" means whatever's left after that day's sale, not a fresh 50%. |
| Teams assume the *water* amount stays constant (grapes) | Ask: "what doesn't disappear when grapes dry out?" — it's the solid matter, not the water. Water is exactly what evaporates. |

**Common instructor mistake:** revealing the walkthrough before a team finishes racing. Hold it — the competitive tension is the point of this format.

**Cut rule:** run Problem 1 (vendor oranges) only; drop the dried-grapes problem if time is short.

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Trace the Table: Varshith, Ayush & Tarun (48–56 min)

### What this activity is

A multi-person ratio-and-percentage word problem, solved by building a variable table together as a class before any arithmetic starts.

### Why it's here

These "three people, two relationships, one average" problems are where students give up — not because the maths is hard, but because they never structure the unknowns first. Trace the Table forces that structuring step.

### Before class

Draw three empty columns on the board: **Tarun | Ayush | Varshith**.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–2:00 | "Who has no comparison — start there." Fill Tarun = x | Call out the variable |
| 2:00–3:30 | Fill Ayush = 1.2x ("20% more than Tarun") | Call out the expression |
| 3:30–4:30 | Fill Varshith = 3.6x ("thrice Ayush") | Call out the expression |
| 4:30–6:30 | Use average = 116 to solve for x, then Varshith | Solve together |
| 6:30–8:00 | Debrief; bonus problem if time remains | Listen / shout an answer |

### Say this

> *"Three people, two relationships, one average. Before anyone touches a calculator, we're filling this table one column at a time — and we start with whoever has nothing to compare to."*

### The problem

> Varshith has thrice as much money as Ayush. Ayush has 20% more than Tarun has. If the average of the amounts they have is Rs 116, how much money does Varshith have?

### Answer

**216.**

Table: Tarun = x, Ayush = 1.2x, Varshith = 3(1.2x) = 3.6x.
Average: (x + 1.2x + 3.6x) / 3 = 116 → 5.8x = 348 → x = 60.
Varshith = 3.6 × 60 = **216**.

### Bonus (only if time remains)

> A student multiplied a number by 3/5 instead of 5/3. What is the percentage error in the calculation?

**Answer: 64%.** Take one shouted answer, confirm, move straight to the exit ticket — don't work the full steps live unless the room finished the main problem with time to spare.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students start with Varshith's column first | It depends on Ayush, which depends on Tarun — reorder: always start from the variable with no dependency. |
| Students write Ayush = Tarun + 20 (flat addition) | They're treating "20% more" as "+20" instead of ×1.2. Point back to the percentage-change formula from Slide Block A. |
| Students set sum = 116 instead of sum/3 = 116 | Remind: average = sum ÷ count. Rewrite the equation together. |

**Common instructor mistake:** solving the equation yourself once the table is built, instead of handing the algebra back to the class. The table-building is the activity — don't rush past it into "teacher solves it."

**Cut rule:** drop the bonus % error problem; the ratio-table problem alone carries the session's content.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Two successive % changes just add up (10% + 10% = 20%) | Percentages "feel" additive from school arithmetic | Sai's example (Slide Block A) — 10% then 10% is 21%, not 20%, because the second hike lands on the bigger number |
| A % increase and the % decrease that undoes it are the same number | 40→60 "looks like" it should reverse at the same rate | The hook and Slide Block A's 40/60/40 kmph illustration — 50% up, only 33.33% down |
| "A is x% more than B" means "B is x% less than A" | Both statements use the same number x | The Pavan/Prudhvi problem — 40% more only means 28.5714% less, because the bases differ |
| To keep expenditure flat after a price rise, cut consumption by the same % | The rise and the needed cut "should" cancel symmetrically | The dal problem — a 10% price rise needs only a 9 1/11% consumption cut, since the cut is calculated on the new, higher price |
| In multi-step % problems, percentages of different remaining amounts can just be added | No visible cue that the "20%" and the "50%" are of different bases | Activity 2's vendor-oranges problem — 20% of 30% and 50% of 24% are not interchangeable with 20% and 50% of the original |
