## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Define proportion as the equality of two ratios and state the product rule (`a:b :: c:d` ⇒ `ad = bc`). *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Distinguish continued proportion (`a:b :: b:c`, three terms) from an ordinary four-term proportion. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the proportion product rule to solve for an unknown term. *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve multi-quantity ratio problems by manipulating ratios with multiplication/division rather than addition/subtraction. *(APPLYING)*
5. Recognise a direct-variation relationship between two changing quantities and set up the proportional relationship between them. *(ANALYZING)*
6. Solve a joint work–rate–time variation problem to determine the additional resources needed when both output and time change. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Prior Knowledge Activation (3–7 min) · ALS: Polling

5 questions on **Session 18 (Ratios)**. Newly authored options; every correct answer and underlying number is drawn from the Ratios deck. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** Two quantities compared using the same units and expressed as `a:b` are called a:
`A` Fraction · `B` Ratio · `C` Percentage · `D` Proportion
→ **B.** *If shaky,* say the definition out loud once — everything today depends on it.

**Q2.** If `x:y = 2:3` and `y:z = 5:7`, what is `x:y:z`?
`A` `2:3:7` · `B` `10:15:21` · `C` `10:15:35` · `D` `4:6:7`
→ **B.** *Targets:* making the common term (`y`) equal before chaining ratios.

**Q3.** The ratio of two quantities is `3:4`. If the first quantity is Rs. 810, what is the second?
`A` Rs. 607.50 · `B` Rs. 1080 · `C` Rs. 972 · `D` Rs. 1215
→ **B.** *Targets:* scaling a two-term ratio from one known value.

**Q4.** 351 bananas are split among three monkeys in the ratio `1/2 : 1/3 : 1/4`. How many did the first monkey get?
`A` 117 · `B` 135 · `C` 162 · `D` 175
→ **C.** *Targets:* converting a fractional ratio to a whole-number ratio via the LCM of the denominators.

**Q5.** *(Analysis)* Rs. 16,940 is divided among A, B, and C such that A gets `3/4` of `(B+C)` and B gets `5/6` of `(A+C)`. What is B's share?
`A` Rs. 6900 · `B` Rs. 7300 · `C` Rs. 7700 · `D` Rs. 8140
→ **C.** *Note the number* — if this one is weak, slow down on the salary/income problems in today's Slide Block A, which use the same "set up the relationship, then solve" skill.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question. Total ~3.75 min for the 5 questions.

## Hook (7–9 min)

Write this on the board, with `x` boxed and nothing else around it:

```
0.75 : x  ::  5 : 8
```

Say: *"This is a real problem from today's deck. By the time we hit the 19-minute mark, every one of you will solve this in under ten seconds, no calculator. Shout a guess — right now, before you know the trick."*

Let two or three guesses land. Don't confirm or deny any of them.

Then: *"You already have ratios cold from last session — Q2 of the poll had you chaining two ratios together by matching up `y`, and that took real work. Today's topic is what happens when you take two ratios and simply declare them equal. That's called a proportion, and it comes with a one-line shortcut that cracks the box on the board in seconds."*

Tie back to the poll: *"Proportion is going to do, in one step, what you just did the long way in Q2."*

## Slide Block A (9–16 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — the deck interleaves a "Recap | Ratios" title card, an agenda slide, four Problems-on-Ratio slides, a Quiz Time marker, then the Proportion and Continued Proportion definition slides. This block groups the ratio-recap problems with the proportion definitions for teaching flow; confirm the live slide order and adjust if it differs. -->

Covers: recap problems on ratio → Proportion (equality of two ratios, `a:b::c:d`, `ad = bc`) → Continued Proportion (`a:b::b:c`, three terms only).

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Warm through with two ratio-recap problems, worked live:** (1) Prudhvi & Manoj's salaries are in ratio `2:3`; after each gets a Rs. 4000 raise the new ratio is `40:57` — Manoj's (original) salary is **Rs. 34000**. (2) The sum of three numbers is 98; ratio of 1st:2nd is `2:3` and 2nd:3rd is `5:8` — using the exact "make the common term equal" method from the warm-up poll, the second number is **30**. Deliberately the same technique as poll Q2 — say that out loud, it's the payoff of the warm-up.
- **Proportion is the equality of two ratios.** `a:b :: c:d` means `a/b = c/d`. Shortcut: `ad = bc` — the product of the outer pair (**extremes**) equals the product of the inner pair (**means**).
- **Continued proportion has only three terms.** `a:b :: b:c` — the middle term `b` is shared. State explicitly this is *not* a four-term proportion in disguise.

**Checkpoint (at 16 min)** — closing the Hook loop:
> *"Board puzzle time. `0.75 : x :: 5 : 8`. Extremes times extremes, means times means — go."*
> **Answer:** `0.75 × 8 = 5 × x` → `6 = 5x` → **`x = 1.2`**.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Think-Pair-Share: Two Ratio Problems (16–23 min)

**ALS format:** Think-Pair-Share. Chosen right after Slide Block A because it just modelled the "make-the-relationship-explicit, then solve" method — this hands the method to students immediately, while it's fresh, on two problems they haven't seen solved yet.

**Setup line:**
> *"Two problems. Ninety seconds alone, then compare with the person next to you — if you disagree, that's useful, resolve it together. I want the answer AND the equation you set up to get there, not just a number."*

**Problem 1:**
> A sum of money is distributed among Varsha, Likhith, Ramesh, and Nandana in the proportion `5:2:4:3`. If Ramesh gets Rs. 1000 more than Nandana, what is Likhith's share?

**Answer:** Ramesh = `4x`, Nandana = `3x`. `4x − 3x = 1000` → `x = 1000`. Likhith = `2x` = **Rs. 2000**.

**Problem 2:**
> Monthly incomes of A and B are in the ratio `4:5`, and their monthly expenses are in the ratio `5:7`. If each of them saves Rs. 2400, find the income of A.

**Answer:** Income `A = 4x`, `B = 5x`; expense `A = 5y`, `B = 7y`. Savings equal: `4x − 5y = 5x − 7y` → `x = 2y`. Substitute: `4(2y) − 5y = 2400` → `y = 800`, `x = 1600`. Income of A = **Rs. 6400**.

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs jump straight to guessing numbers | Stop them: *"What's `x` standing for? Write the equation first."* |
| Problem 1 pairs subtract shares directly | Ask: *"Whose shares did you subtract — are those the right two people?"* |
| Problem 2 stalls because two unknowns feels like too much | Point out both equations describe the *same* saved amount — that's the bridge that eliminates one variable. |

**Debrief line:**
> *"The setup is the skill, not the arithmetic. Once the equation was right, both problems solved themselves."*

**Cut rule:** Drop Problem 1, keep Problem 2 and the full debrief — it's the richer two-variable setup and generalises better.

## Slide Block B (23–30 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — deck order is Variation intro → a proportion problem (already pulled forward into Block A's checkpoint) → the race problem → the diesel/engine problem → the programmers problem. This block delivers Variation plus the diesel problem, holds the race problem back as the checkpoint, and hands the programmers problem to ALS Activity 2. -->

Covers: Variation (direct variation: engine capacity ↔ fuel capacity, distance ↔ fuel required) → a direct-variation word problem (diesel and engine capacity).

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Variation is proportion applied to two quantities that change together.** If CC (engine capacity) increases, petrol capacity increases; if km (distance) increases, petrol required increases. Both move in the *same* direction — that's **direct variation**.
- **Direct variation problem, worked live:** 60 litres of diesel is needed to travel 600 km using an 800 cc engine. Diesel required varies directly with both distance *and* engine capacity. For 800 km with a 1200 cc engine, diesel required = **120 litres**.
- **Say explicitly:** the deck's speaker notes flag Direct, Indirect, and Joint variation as the three types, but only direct-variation examples are captured in this deck's text — flag that inverse variation exists but isn't worked content today.

**Checkpoint (at 30 min)** — cold-call, deliberately testing a misconception the deck itself calls out:
> *"In a 1000 m race, A beats B by 200 m and A beats C by 300 m. By how many metres will B beat C?"*
> **Wrong-but-tempting answer:** `300 − 200 = 100 m`. **Correct answer: 125 m.** Method: A:B = `1000:800`, A:C = `1000:700`, so B:C = `800:700 = 8:7`. When B covers 1000 m, C covers `875` m → B beats C by **125 m**. State the deck's own rule: *"Addition and subtraction are not acceptable for manipulating ratios — prefer multiplication and division."*

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Trace the Table: The Programmers Problem (30–40 min)

**ALS format:** Guided Table Build — one dense joint-variation word problem, solved by building a four-row table on the board together, one row at a time; nobody states the final number until the table is filled. Chosen as the closing activity because this is the session's hardest problem — it varies directly with the amount of work *and* inversely with the time available, at once — and students who can quote "direct" and "inverse" variation separately usually freeze the first time both show up together.

**Setup line:**
> *"One problem, one table, four rows. Nobody says the final number until all four rows are filled and everyone agrees on each one. Row 1 — go."*

**The problem:**
> A software programmer provider has trained its manpower such that 36 programmers could write 36 software programs of similar nature in 36 hours. The company has received an order for 84 software programs of similar nature in 24 hours. How many additional programmers should the company employ for this project?

**The table**

| Step | Quantity | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Man-hours to write one program | `36 programmers × 36 hours ÷ 36 programs = 36` man-hours per program |
| 2 | Man-hours needed for the new order of 84 programs | `84 × 36 = 3024` man-hours |
| 3 | Programmers needed to deliver that in 24 hours | `3024 ÷ 24 = 126` programmers |
| 4 | Additional programmers needed beyond the current 36 | `126 − 36 = ` **90** |

**The trap to name explicitly:** *"Why can't you just say 84 is 2.33× of 36 programs, so scale 36 programmers by 2.33?"* **Answer:** Because time also changed — from 36 hours down to 24. Scaling programmers by the programs ratio alone ignores that there's now *less* time, which needs *more* people. The man-hours step is what correctly folds both changes in at once.

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| A student states the final answer before the table is full | *"Hold it — prove it row by row before I write it down."* |
| Row 1 stalls because "man-hours" is unfamiliar | Reframe: *"If it takes 36 people 36 hours to do 36 programs, how many hours would ONE person need for ONE program?"* → 36. |
| Someone tries the direct-scaling shortcut (84/36 × 36) | Let them get the wrong number, then run the time-check question — the mismatch is the teaching moment. |

**Debrief line:**
> *"Direct and inverse variation almost never show up alone in a real problem. The table is what keeps them from blurring into one wrong shortcut."*

**Cut rule:** Supply Row 1, have the class do Rows 2–4, keep the trap question and skip the open debrief.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| B beats C by `300 − 200 = 100 m` in the race problem | Subtracting margins feels linear and intuitive | Slide Block B's checkpoint — running the multiplicative method |
| In `a:b::c:d`, any diagonal pair can be multiplied together | The rule "cross-multiply" is remembered without the extremes/means labelling | Naming the outer pair (extremes) and inner pair (means) explicitly before solving |
| Continued proportion `a:b::b:c` secretly has four terms | Every other proportion example they've seen has four | The deck's own explicit statement: "only three terms" |
| Scaling programmers by the programs ratio alone is enough | Only one quantity (programs) is salient; time is easy to overlook | ALS Activity 2's trap question |
