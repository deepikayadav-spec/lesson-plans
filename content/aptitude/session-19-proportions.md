## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Define proportion as the equality of two ratios and state the product rule (`a:b :: c:d` ⇒ `ad = bc`, extremes-times-extremes equals means-times-means). *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Distinguish continued proportion (`a:b :: b:c`, three terms) from an ordinary four-term proportion. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Apply the proportion product rule to solve for an unknown term (e.g., `0.75 : x :: 5 : 8`). *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve multi-quantity ratio problems — salary, income-and-expense, race-margin problems — by manipulating ratios with multiplication/division rather than addition/subtraction. *(APPLYING)*
5. Recognise a direct-variation relationship between two changing quantities (e.g., engine capacity and fuel required) and set up the proportional relationship between them. *(ANALYZING)*
6. Solve a joint work–rate–time variation problem to determine the additional resources needed when both output and time change. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Prior Knowledge Activation (0–7 min)

7 questions on **Session 18 (Ratios)**. Newly authored options; every correct answer and underlying number is drawn from the Ratios deck. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** Two quantities compared using the same units and expressed as `a:b` are called a:
`A` Fraction · `B` Ratio · `C` Percentage · `D` Proportion
→ **B.** *Targets:* the basic definition from Session 18. *If this is shaky,* say the definition out loud once before moving on — everything today depends on it.

**Q2.** If `x:y = 2:3` and `y:z = 5:7`, what is `x:y:z`?
`A` `2:3:7` · `B` `10:15:21` · `C` `10:15:35` · `D` `4:6:7`
→ **B.** *Targets:* making the common term (`y`) equal before chaining ratios — Session 18's "Reverse-N" method. *Misconception (C):* scaling only the second ratio's second term and forgetting the first term of `y` must match too.

**Q3.** The ratio of two quantities is `3:4`. If the first quantity is Rs. 810, what is the second?
`A` Rs. 607.50 · `B` Rs. 1080 · `C` Rs. 972 · `D` Rs. 1215
→ **B.** *Targets:* scaling a two-term ratio from one known value.

**Q4.** 351 bananas are split among three monkeys in the ratio `1/2 : 1/3 : 1/4`. How many did the first monkey get?
`A` 117 · `B` 135 · `C` 162 · `D` 175
→ **C.** *Targets:* converting a fractional ratio to a whole-number ratio via the LCM of the denominators before dividing the total. *If >40% miss this:* re-run the LCM step on the board — it recurs constantly in ratio-distribution problems.

**Q5.** Vijay has coins of Rs. 1, 50p, and 25p in the ratio `12:10:7`. The total worth of his coins is Rs. 75. How many 25p coins does he have?
`A` 20 · `B` 24 · `C` 28 · `D` 35
→ **C.** *Targets:* weighting a ratio by value-per-unit before solving, not just splitting the total by the raw ratio.

**Q6.** The ratio of water to milk in a 30-litre mixture is `7:3`. How much water must be added to make the ratio `6:1`?
`A` 20 L · `B` 27 L · `C` 30 L · `D` 33 L
→ **D.** *Targets:* mixture problems where only one component changes — milk stays fixed, water is solved for.

**Q7.** *(Analysis)* Rs. 16,940 is divided among A, B, and C such that A gets `3/4` of `(B+C)` and B gets `5/6` of `(A+C)`. What is B's share?
`A` Rs. 6900 · `B` Rs. 7300 · `C` Rs. 7700 · `D` Rs. 8140
→ **C.** *Targets:* converting "part = fraction of the rest" statements into a clean part-ratio before dividing the total. *Note the number* — if this one is weak, slow down on the salary/income problems in today's Slide Block A, which use the same "set up the relationship, then solve" skill.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, project the distribution after each. Total 7 min including reads.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Write this on the board, with `x` boxed and nothing else around it:

```
0.75 : x  ::  5 : 8
```

Say: *"This is a real problem from today's deck. By the time we hit the 22-minute mark, every one of you will solve this in under ten seconds, no calculator. Shout a guess — right now, before you know the trick."*

Let two or three guesses land. Don't confirm or deny any of them.

Then: *"You already have ratios cold from last session — Q2 of the poll had you chaining two ratios together by matching up `y`, and that took real work. Today's topic is what happens when you take two ratios and simply declare them equal. That's called a proportion, and it comes with a one-line shortcut that cracks the box on the board in seconds."*

Tie back to the poll: *"Proportion is going to do, in one step, what you just did the long way in Q2."*

## Slide Block A (10–22 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — the deck interleaves a "Recap | Ratios" title card, an agenda slide, four Problems-on-Ratio slides, a Quiz Time marker, then the Proportion and Continued Proportion definition slides. Covers: recap problems on ratio → Proportion (equality of two ratios, `a:b::c:d`, `ad = bc`) → Continued Proportion (`a:b::b:c`, three terms only).

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Warm through with two ratio-recap problems, worked live on the slides:** (1) Prudhvi & Manoj's salaries are in ratio `2:3`; after each gets a Rs. 4000 raise the new ratio is `40:57` — Manoj's (original) salary is **Rs. 34000**. (2) The sum of three numbers is 98; ratio of 1st:2nd is `2:3` and 2nd:3rd is `5:8` — using the exact "make the common term equal" method from the warm-up poll, the second number is **30**. These are deliberately the same technique as poll Q2 — say that out loud, it's the payoff of the warm-up.
- **Proportion is the equality of two ratios.** `a:b :: c:d` means `a/b = c/d`. The deck's shortcut: `ad = bc` — the product of the outer pair (**extremes**) equals the product of the inner pair (**means**; the deck's own slide text says "intermediate terms" — use that wording if it's on screen, but call them means, since that's the term students will meet elsewhere).
- **Continued proportion has only three terms.** `a:b :: b:c` — the middle term `b` is shared. State explicitly, as the deck does, that this is *not* a four-term proportion in disguise; `b` is doing double duty.

**Checkpoint (at 22 min)** — cold-call, closing the Hook loop:
> *"Board puzzle time. `0.75 : x :: 5 : 8`. Extremes times extremes, means times means — go."*
> **Answer:** `0.75 × 8 = 5 × x` → `6 = 5x` → **`x = 1.2`**.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Think–Pair–Share: Two Ratio Problems (22–29 min)

### What this activity is

Students work two multi-step ratio problems in pairs before any answer is shown, then the pairs report out and you run the correct method live. Both problems are taken verbatim from the deck's Problems-on-Ratio slides.

### Why it's here

Slide Block A just modelled the "make-the-relationship-explicit, then solve" method on two problems. This activity hands the method to the students immediately, while it's fresh, on two problems they haven't seen solved yet.

### Before class

Have both problems ready on a slide or the board, one at a time.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal Problem 1 | Read |
| 0:30–2:30 | Silent, then pair up | Solve individually, then compare with partner |
| 2:30–3:30 | Take the answer from two pairs, run the method live | Answer, watch |
| 3:30–4:00 | Reveal Problem 2 | Read |
| 4:00–6:00 | Pairs solve | Solve |
| 6:00–7:00 | Take the answer, run the method live, debrief | Answer, listen |

### Say this

> *"Two problems. Ninety seconds alone, then compare with the person next to you — if you disagree, that's useful, resolve it together. I want the answer AND the equation you set up to get there, not just a number."*

### Problem 1

> A sum of money is distributed among Varsha, Likhith, Ramesh, and Nandana in the proportion `5:2:4:3`. If Ramesh gets Rs. 1000 more than Nandana, what is Likhith's share?

**Answer:** Ramesh = `4x`, Nandana = `3x`. `4x − 3x = 1000` → `x = 1000`. Likhith = `2x` = **Rs. 2000**.

### Problem 2

> Monthly incomes of A and B are in the ratio `4:5`, and their monthly expenses are in the ratio `5:7`. If each of them saves Rs. 2400, find the income of A.

**Answer:** Income `A = 4x`, `B = 5x`; expense `A = 5y`, `B = 7y`. Savings equal: `4x − 5y = 5x − 7y` → `x = 2y`. Substitute: `4(2y) − 5y = 2400` → `3y = 2400` → `y = 800`, `x = 1600`. Income of A = `4x` = **Rs. 6400**.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs jump straight to guessing numbers | Stop them: *"What's `x` standing for? Write the equation first."* The setup is the skill, not the arithmetic. |
| Problem 1 pairs subtract shares directly (`5−3=1000` type shortcuts) | Ask: *"Whose shares did you subtract — are those the right two people?"* Ramesh and Nandana, not Varsha. |
| Problem 2 stalls because two unknowns feels like too much | Point out both equations describe the *same* saved amount — that's the bridge that eliminates one variable. |
| Running long | Do Problem 2 only — it's the richer two-variable setup and generalises better. |

**Common instructor mistake:** solving it for them the moment a pair looks stuck. Let the silent 90 seconds run its full length before intervening — the productive struggle is the point.

**Cut rule:** Drop Problem 1, keep Problem 2 and the full debrief.

Covers: Variation (direct variation: engine capacity ↔ fuel capacity, distance ↔ fuel required) → a direct-variation word problem (diesel and engine capacity).

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Variation is proportion applied to two quantities that change together.** The deck's own examples: if CC (engine capacity) increases, petrol capacity increases; if km (distance) increases, petrol required increases. Both move in the *same* direction — that's **direct variation**.
- **Direct variation problem, worked live:** 60 litres of diesel is needed to travel 600 km using an 800 cc engine. Diesel required varies directly with both distance *and* engine capacity. Scaling both factors: for 800 km with a 1200 cc engine, diesel required = **120 litres**.
- **Say explicitly:** the deck's speaker notes flag Direct, Indirect, and Joint variation as the three types, but only direct-variation examples are captured in this deck's text — flag to students that inverse (indirect) variation exists (e.g., speed and time for a fixed distance) but is not this session's worked content; don't manufacture a full example on the fly.

**Checkpoint (at 41 min)** — cold-call, deliberately testing a misconception the deck itself calls out:
> *"In a 1000 m race, A beats B by 200 m and A beats C by 300 m. By how many metres will B beat C?"*
> **Wrong-but-tempting answer:** `300 − 200 = 100 m` (subtracting the margins). **Correct answer: 125 m.** Method: A:B = `1000:800`, A:C = `1000:700`, so B:C = `800:700 = 8:7`. When B covers 1000 m, C covers `(7/8)×1000 = 875` m → B beats C by **125 m**. State the deck's own rule out loud: *"Addition and subtraction are not acceptable for manipulating ratios — prefer multiplication and division."*

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Trace the Table: The Programmers Problem (41–51 min)

### What this activity is

One dense joint-variation word problem, solved by building a four-row table on the board together, one row at a time, instead of jumping straight to an answer. Nobody is allowed to state the final number until the table is filled.

### Why it's here

This is the session's hardest problem — it varies directly with the amount of work *and* inversely with the time available, at once. Students who can quote "direct variation" and "inverse variation" separately usually freeze the first time both show up in the same question. Building the table forces the two effects apart before combining them.

### Before class

Draw a blank 4-row, 2-column table on the board: **Step** | **Value**. Have the problem statement on screen.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal problem | Read |
| 0:30–1:30 | Ask for Step 1 only, wait | Propose the man-hours-per-program figure |
| 1:30–5:00 | Fill Steps 2–4 one at a time, calling on a different student each time | Compute each step out loud |
| 5:00–8:00 | Confirm final answer, run the "why not just scale by 84/36" trap | Check their own working |
| 8:00–10:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"One problem, one table, four rows. Nobody says the final number until all four rows are filled and everyone agrees on each one. Row 1 — go."*

### The problem

> A software programmer provider has trained its manpower such that 36 programmers could write 36 software programs of similar nature in 36 hours. The company has received an order for 84 software programs of similar nature in 24 hours. How many additional programmers should the company employ for this project?

### The table

| Step | Quantity | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Man-hours to write one program (from the original 36/36/36 setup) | `36 programmers × 36 hours ÷ 36 programs = 36` man-hours per program |
| 2 | Man-hours needed for the new order of 84 programs | `84 × 36 = 3024` man-hours |
| 3 | Programmers needed to deliver that in 24 hours | `3024 ÷ 24 = 126` programmers |
| 4 | Additional programmers needed beyond the current 36 | `126 − 36 = ` **90** |

### The trap to name explicitly

> *"Why can't you just say 84 is 2.33× of 36 programs, so scale 36 programmers by 2.33?"*

**Answer:** Because time also changed — from 36 hours down to 24. Scaling programmers by the programs ratio alone ignores that there's now *less* time, which needs *more* people, not fewer. The man-hours step (Row 1–2) is what correctly folds both changes in at once.

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| A student states the final answer before the table is full | Acknowledge it, then say *"Hold it — prove it row by row before I write it down."* |
| Row 1 stalls because "man-hours" is unfamiliar | Reframe: *"If it takes 36 people 36 hours to do 36 programs, how many hours would ONE person need for ONE program?"* → 36. |
| Someone tries the direct-scaling shortcut (84/36 × 36) | Let them get the wrong number, then run the time-check question above — the mismatch is the teaching moment, don't head it off early. |
| Running long | Skip straight to filling Rows 2–4 as a class after you supply Row 1 yourself; keep the trap question, it's non-negotiable. |

**Common instructor mistake:** filling the table yourself to save time. The slow, row-by-row build is what separates "direct" from "inverse" in students' heads — narrating it faster defeats the purpose.

**Cut rule:** Supply Row 1, have the class do Rows 2–4, keep the trap question and skip the open debrief.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (51–60 min)

**Exit ticket** — on paper or in chat before anyone leaves:

> State the proportion rule in one line. Then use it to solve: `0.75 : x :: 5 : 8`. What is `x`?
> **Answers:** In a proportion `a:b::c:d`, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means (`ad = bc`). `0.75 × 8 = 5 × x` → **`x = 1.2`**.

Scan responses on the way out. Anyone still cross-multiplying the wrong pair (e.g., `0.75×5 = x×8`) needs the extremes/means labelling re-shown at the start of Session 20.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| B beats C by `300 − 200 = 100 m` in the race problem | Subtracting margins feels linear and intuitive | The Slide Block B checkpoint — running the multiplicative method and stating the deck's own rule: addition/subtraction is not acceptable for manipulating ratios |
| In `a:b::c:d`, any diagonal pair can be multiplied together | The rule "cross-multiply" is remembered without the extremes/means labelling | Naming the outer pair (extremes) and inner pair (means) explicitly on the board before solving the checkpoint puzzle |
| Continued proportion `a:b::b:c` secretly has four terms | Every other proportion example they've seen has four | The deck's own explicit statement, repeated aloud: "only three terms, `a`, `b`, and `c`" |
| Scaling programmers by the programs ratio alone is enough (ignore the time change) | Only one quantity (programs) is salient; the second changing quantity (time) is easy to overlook | Activity 2's trap question — naming that time also changed, and that man-hours is what captures both changes at once |
