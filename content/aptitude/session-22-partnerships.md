## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. State that when all partners invest for the same period, profit is shared strictly in the ratio of their capital. *(REMEMBERING)*
2. Explain why two partners who invest equal capital for different durations cannot receive equal profit shares. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
3. Compute each partner's profit share from a given set of capitals (two or three partners) using the ratio-of-investment method. *(APPLYING)*
4. Apply the capital × time (investment × duration) rule to find a partner's share when an investment changes mid-year — including a partner joining late or withdrawing part of their capital. *(APPLYING)*
5. Solve partnership problems where a fixed amount is deducted from each partner's share before the remainder is split in a given ratio. *(APPLYING)*
6. Reverse-engineer the investment ratio of partners from a given profit-sharing ratio and their respective investment durations. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Prior Knowledge Activation (0–7 min)

7 questions on **Session 21 — Mixtures and Alligations**. Newly authored, grounded in that deck's real numbers. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** What is the name of the method used to find the mixing ratio when two ingredients of different cost or quality are combined to make a mixture of some intermediate value?
`A` Alligation rule · `B` Permutation rule · `C` Probability rule · `D` HCF rule
→ **A.** *Targets:* naming last session's core tool, before today reuses the same ratio logic in a business context.

**Q2.** Two varieties of pulses cost Rs.15/kg and Rs.20/kg. They are mixed to get a mixture worth Rs.16.50/kg. What is the mixing ratio (cheaper : dearer)?
`A` 3 : 7 · `B` 7 : 3 · `C` 1 : 2 · `D` 2 : 1
→ **B.** *Targets:* recall of the session's opening worked example.

**Q3.** Water is mixed with milk costing Rs.12/litre to get a mixture worth Rs.8/litre. In the alligation calculation, what price is water taken to be?
`A` Rs.8/litre · `B` Rs.4/litre · `C` Rs.0/litre · `D` Rs.12/litre
→ **C.** *Targets:* the deck's own flagged trap — students forget water is free in this method. *If >40% miss this,* re-run the water/milk alligation cross on the board before moving on.

**Q4.** A shopkeeper has 1000 kg of sugar. Part is sold at 8% profit, the rest at 18% profit, and he gains 14% overall. How much was sold at 18% profit?
`A` 400 kg · `B` 500 kg · `C` 600 kg · `D` 700 kg
→ **C.** *Targets:* alligation applied to profit percentages, not just prices.

**Q5.** Tea worth Rs.126/kg and Rs.135/kg is mixed with a third variety in the ratio 1:1:2. The resulting mixture is worth Rs.153/kg. What is the price of the third variety?
`A` Rs.162 · `B` Rs.170 · `C` Rs.175.5 · `D` Rs.180
→ **C.** *Targets:* three-way weighted-average reasoning, the skill today's reverse-ratio problem also leans on.

**Q6.** Rice at Rs.9/kg is mixed with a second quality in the ratio 1:3, giving a mixture worth Rs.12/kg. What is the rate of the second quality?
`A` Rs.11/kg · `B` Rs.13/kg · `C` Rs.15/kg · `D` Rs.17/kg
→ **B.** *Targets:* solving for an unknown rate given a known ratio and mean.

**Q7.** *(MSQ — select all that apply)* Which of these ratio answers from last session are correct?
`A` Pulses (Rs.15 & Rs.20) → mixture at Rs.16.50 gives ratio 7:3
`B` Water : Milk (Rs.12/L) → mixture at Rs.8/L gives ratio 1:2
`C` Sugar (Rs.30 & Rs.40/kg) sold at Rs.36/kg with 10% gain gives ratio 8:3
`D` Rice (Rs.20/kg) mixed with rice (Rs.30/kg) to get Rs.26/kg gives ratio 3:2
→ **A, B, C.** *(D is false — the real answer is 2:3, cheaper:dearer, not 3:2.)* *Targets:* whether students remember not just the numbers but which side of the ratio belongs to which ingredient — the same direction-of-ratio care they'll need today when assigning shares to named partners.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say this, writing the numbers on the board as you go:

> *"Two of your friends decide to open a food stall together. Friend A puts in Rs.1,20,000. Friend B puts in Rs.1,50,000. At the end of the year, they've made a profit of Rs.27,000. Show of hands — who thinks they should just split it fifty-fifty, half each?"*

Let hands go up. Take one "yes" and one "no" reason out loud.

> *"Real partnerships almost never split it evenly. The size of your investment decides your share — and, as you'll see in about fifteen minutes, so does something most people forget entirely: how long you kept that money in the business. Yesterday you used ratios to mix pulses and sugar profits. Today the exact same ratio tool decides how business partners split real money."*

> *"By the end of this slide block, you will be able to work out exactly how much each of these two friends should walk away with. Let's build the rule."*

## Slide Block A (10–22 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — the deck's slide-by-slide timing isn't tagged in the text extraction; boundary chosen so Block A ends on the capital×time concept before students practise it themselves -->

Covers: Partnerships title/agenda slide → Example 1 (two partners, equal time) → Example 2 (three partners, equal time) → Example 3 (capital changes mid-year, with a partial withdrawal).

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Beat 1 — Equal time, equal treatment.** A and B invest Rs.1,20,000 and Rs.1,50,000 for the full year, profit Rs.27,000. Ratio 120000:150000 = 4:5, sum 9, unit = 3000. **A = Rs.12,000, B = Rs.15,000.** This resolves the Hook — reveal it here, not before.
- **Beat 2 — Same idea, three partners.** A, B, C invest Rs.1,20,000, Rs.1,80,000, Rs.1,50,000 for the full year, profit Rs.27,000. Ratio 4:6:5, sum 15, unit = 1800. **A = Rs.7,200, B = Rs.10,800, C = Rs.9,000.** Say plainly: "same rule, one more partner."
- **Beat 3 — The rule breaks if time isn't equal.** A invests Rs.1,00,000 from day one (12 months). B invests Rs.2,00,000 after 3 months (so for 9 months) and then withdraws half of it after month 9 (so Rs.1,00,000 for the last 3 months). C invests Rs.3,00,000 after 9 months (3 months). Annual profit Rs.1,20,000. Find B's share.
  - A's capital×time = 1,00,000 × 12 = 12,00,000
  - B's capital×time = (2,00,000 × 6) + (1,00,000 × 3) = 12,00,000 + 3,00,000 = 15,00,000
  - C's capital×time = 3,00,000 × 3 = 9,00,000
  - Total = 36,00,000. B's share = 1,20,000 × 15,00,000⁄36,00,000 = **Rs.50,000.**
  - State the rule out loud as you close this beat: *"Profit share depends on capital **and** time invested — not capital alone."*

**Checkpoint (at 22 min)** — cold-call two students:
> *"A friend says: 'B put in double A's capital at some point, so B should always get double A's share, no matter what.' Is that true here? Why or why not?"*
> **Answer:** No. It's only true if both invested for the same length of time. B's Rs.2,00,000 was only fully in for 6 months and then halved — B's effective capital×time (15,00,000) is not simply double A's (12,00,000), so B's share (Rs.50,000) isn't double A's share either.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Think–Pair–Share (22–27 min)

### What this activity is

Two fresh worked problems from the deck, given cold with no method shown. Students solve solo, compare with a partner, then one pair per problem defends their answer to the class before you reveal the real figures.

### Why it's here

Slide Block A modelled the capital-ratio method three times. This is the first time students apply it themselves, on numbers they haven't seen broken down for them.

### Before class

Have both problems ready on a slide or the board, without the answers visible.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal both problems | Read |
| 0:30–2:00 | Silent solo attempt | Solve individually |
| 2:00–3:00 | "Compare with your neighbour" | Pair up, reconcile answers |
| 3:00–4:30 | Call on two pairs, one per problem | Defend their answer |
| 4:30–5:00 | Reveal and debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Two problems. Solo for ninety seconds — no talking. Then compare with the person next to you. I'll call on two pairs to defend their number before I show you the real one."*

### The problems

**Problem 1.** Three partners A, B, C invest Rs.2,100, Rs.2,400 and Rs.2,700 respectively. Find A's share out of an annual profit of Rs.2,520.

**Problem 2.** Rs.5,783 is divided among A, B and C such that if Rs.28, Rs.37 and Rs.18 are deducted from their respective shares, the remainder is in the ratio 4:6:9. Find A's share.

### Answers

| # | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ratio 2100:2400:2700 = 7:8:9, sum 24, unit = 105. A = 7 × 105 | **Rs.735** |
| 2 | Total deducted = 28+37+18 = 83. Remainder = 5783 − 83 = 5700. Ratio sum 4+6+9 = 19, unit = 300. A's remainder-share = 4 × 300 = 1200. **A's full share = 1200 + 28** | **Rs.1,228** |

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs get Problem 1 fast but stall on Problem 2 | Ask: *"Before you can split anything into a ratio, what has to happen to the Rs.5,783 first?"* — steer them to subtract the deductions before applying the ratio. |
| A pair reports A's share as **1200** (forgetting to add back Rs.28) | This is the exact trap. Don't correct immediately — ask the rest of the room to check the arithmetic; let a peer catch it. |
| Room finishes both in under 3 minutes | Ask *why* the two problems needed different first steps — that's the transferable skill, not the arithmetic. |
| Running long | Do Problem 1 only; assign Problem 2 as part of tonight's homework instead. |

**Common instructor mistake:** revealing Problem 2's answer as "1228" without walking through *why* you add the 28 back. The deduct-then-restore step is the entire teaching point.

**Cut rule:** Drop Problem 2, keep Problem 1 — it directly reinforces Slide Block A's method with fresh numbers.

## Classroom Quiz (27–34 min)

> **Classroom Quiz: not yet available — add once question bank exists for this topic.**

Time reallocated to a **live walkthrough of this deck's own "Quiz Time" slides** — the closest grounded substitute. These are the deck's own MCQs (not a platform bank); run them the same way you'd run a classroom quiz — cold-call, poll for A/B/C/D, then reveal and explain.

**Q1** *(deck slide 11)*
A and B invest in a business in the ratio 3:2. 5% of the total profit goes to charity, and A's share is Rs.855. What is the total profit?
- A) 1425 · B) **1500** · C) 1537.5 · D) 1576

> **Working:** Only 95% of the profit is shared between A and B. A's fraction of the *total* profit = 0.95 × 3/5 = 0.57. Total profit = 855 ⁄ 0.57 = **Rs.1,500.**
> **If they pick A) 1425:** they divided 855 by 3/5 alone and forgot the 5%-to-charity deduction entirely. Re-run the fraction out loud: "95% first, then split it 3:2."

**Q2** *(deck slide 12)*
A, B, C together subscribe Rs.50,000 for a business. A subscribes Rs.4,000 more than B, and B subscribes Rs.5,000 more than C. Out of a total profit of Rs.35,000, what does A receive?
- A) 8400 · B) 11900 · C) 13600 · D) **14700**

> **Working:** Let C = x. Then B = x+5000, A = x+9000. Sum: 3x + 14000 = 50000 → x = 12000. So C=12000, B=17000, A=21000 → ratio 21:17:12, sum 50. A's share = 35000 × 21/50 = **Rs.14,700.**
> **If they pick C) 13600 or B) 11900:** they likely set up the offsets in the wrong direction (subtracting instead of adding, or attaching the offset to the wrong partner). Rebuild the algebra on the board: "subscribe is nothing but capital invested" — the deck's own hint.

**Debrief line:**
> *"Notice both of these still come back to the same rule — figure out what fraction of the total profit is actually being shared, and what ratio it's shared in. Everything else is bookkeeping."*

## Slide Block B (34–44 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred — this is the last substantive teaching slide before the deck's closing "Quiz Time" divider, feedback, and thank-you slides -->

Covers: the reverse problem — given the **profit ratio** and each partner's **investment duration**, find the **investment ratio**.

**Beat to emphasise**

- **Beat — Running the rule backwards.** Three partners share profit in the ratio 5:7:8. They invested for 14, 8, and 7 months respectively. Since Profit ratio = Investment ratio × Time ratio (forward direction), the reverse must **divide**, not multiply: Investment ratio = Profit ratio ÷ Time ratio.
  - A: 5⁄14 · B: 7⁄8 · C: 8⁄7
  - Common denominator (LCM of 14, 8, 7) = 56.
  - A: 5⁄14 × 56 = 20 · B: 7⁄8 × 56 = 49 · C: 8⁄7 × 56 = 64
  - **Investment ratio = 20 : 49 : 64.**
- **Verify it live, out loud, with the class:** multiply the investment ratio back by the time periods — 20×14=280, 49×8=392, 64×7=448 — divide through by 56 and you land back on 5:7:8. *"That check is what tells you the answer is right — always run it if you have the time."*

**Checkpoint (at 44 min)** — show hands:
> *"If I'd multiplied the profit ratio by the time instead of dividing, would I get a bigger or smaller number for A compared to 20?"*
> **Answer:** Bigger — multiplying instead of dividing by the time ratio inflates every term instead of correcting for it. This is exactly the reverse of the forward rule, so the operation must reverse too.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Spot the Bug (44–50 min)

### What this activity is

A worked solution — with a deliberate, realistic error — is put on the board. Students must find *what's wrong with the method*, not just disagree with the final number.

### Why it's here

This is the session's single most important misconception, and the deck itself flags it directly (Example 3's hint: "profit depends on time period of investment"). Students need to catch this error themselves, not just be told about it.

### Before class

Write "Student X's solution" on the board exactly as below, presented as if it were a genuine submitted answer.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Put up the buggy solution, setup line | Read |
| 0:30–2:00 | Wait, silent | Check the working line by line |
| 2:00–5:00 | Take answers, run the correct method live for comparison | Explain the flaw |
| 5:00–6:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"This is a real answer someone gave for the mid-year problem from Slide Block A — A invests Rs.1,00,000 for 12 months, B invests Rs.2,00,000 then withdraws half after month 9, C invests Rs.3,00,000 for the last 3 months, annual profit Rs.1,20,000. Find the flaw."*

### The buggy solution

> *"A invested Rs.1,00,000. B invested Rs.2,00,000. C invested Rs.3,00,000. Ratio = 1:2:3, sum = 6. B's share = 1,20,000 × 2⁄6 = Rs.40,000."*

### Answer

**The flaw:** the "solution" uses only the capital amounts and ignores how long each amount was actually invested. B's Rs.2,00,000 was not in the business for the full year — it was in for 6 months at Rs.2,00,000, then 3 months at Rs.1,00,000 after the withdrawal. Using capital×time (as done in Slide Block A), B's correct total is 15,00,000 against a grand total of 36,00,000, giving a share of **Rs.50,000** — not Rs.40,000.

> *"Rs.40,000 is what you get when you split by capital alone. Rs.50,000 is what you get when you split by capital **and** time. That ten-thousand-rupee gap is the whole lesson of today's session."*

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students say "the ratio 1:2:3 is just wrong" without saying why | Push: *"Wrong because of what, specifically?"* Make them name capital×time, not just reject the number. |
| Someone defends Rs.40,000 | Ask them to recompute A's capital×time (12,00,000) and B's raw capital×time before the withdrawal (also 12,00,000 for 6 months) — the numbers should look suspiciously equal, which is the giveaway that the naive ratio understates B. |
| Room solves it instantly | Good — ask them to also check C's contribution stayed correct in the buggy version (it happens to be right there, since C never changed capital) — show that the bug doesn't wreck *every* term, only the changing one. |
| Running long | Skip the verification of C; keep the B-only comparison — that's the core of the activity. |

**Common instructor mistake:** re-deriving the correct answer before letting students articulate the flaw in their own words. Let them name it first.

**Cut rule:** If very short on time, skip the "When it goes wrong" extensions and go straight from reveal to the debrief line.

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Trace the Table (50–57 min)

### What this activity is

The class rebuilds, row by row on the board, the profit→investment reverse-calculation from Slide Block B — but this time *you* don't fill in the table, they call out each cell.

### Why it's here

Slide Block B was delivered as a worked example. This activity is the students' only chance to run the reverse-ratio method with their own hands before the session ends.

### Before class

Draw an empty table on the board with headers: Partner | Profit ratio | Time (months) | Profit ÷ Time | ×56 (LCM) | Investment ratio.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Reveal the empty table, setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–1:00 | Fill Partner / Profit ratio / Time columns from dictation | Call out the three known values per partner |
| 1:00–4:00 | Point at each remaining empty cell in turn | Compute and call out that one cell |
| 4:00–6:00 | Fill LCM step together, complete last column | Confirm the final ratio |
| 6:00–7:00 | Verification + debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Same problem as Slide Block B — profit ratio 5:7:8, months 14, 8, 7 — but this time I'm not solving it. You call out every cell, one at a time, and I write exactly what you say."*

### The table, completed

| Partner | Profit ratio | Time (months) | Profit ÷ Time | × 56 | Investment ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 14 | 5/14 | 20 | **20** |
| B | 7 | 8 | 7/8 | 49 | **49** |
| C | 8 | 7 | 8/7 | 64 | **64** |

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Someone calls out Profit × Time instead of Profit ÷ Time | This is the expected trap — point back at the checkpoint from Slide Block B: "which direction does the arrow run when we reverse it?" |
| Class gets stuck finding the LCM of 14, 8, 7 | Build it together: 14=2×7, 8=2³, 7=7 → LCM needs 2³×7 = 56. Don't just state 56. |
| Table fills in under 4 minutes | Ask the class to verify by multiplying back (20×14, 49×8, 64×7) and reducing — the same check from Slide Block B. |
| Running long | Skip the LCM derivation and simply state 56, then fill the last two columns quickly. |

**Common instructor mistake:** letting one confident student call out every cell. Rotate — point at a different student per row so the whole room stays accountable.

**Cut rule:** If very short on time, do only the Profit ÷ Time column live and state the final ×56 ratio yourself.

## Exit Ticket + Homework (57–60 min)

**Exit ticket** — on paper or in chat before anyone leaves:

> In today's mid-year investment problem (A: Rs.1,00,000 for 12 months; B: Rs.2,00,000 for 6 months then Rs.1,00,000 for the last 3 months; C: Rs.3,00,000 for 3 months; annual profit Rs.1,20,000), B's correct share is Rs.50,000 — not Rs.40,000. In one or two sentences, explain what mistake produces Rs.40,000, and why it's wrong.
> **Answer:** Rs.40,000 comes from splitting the profit using raw capital alone (ratio 1:2:3, ignoring time). It's wrong because B did not keep the full Rs.2,00,000 invested for the whole year — profit share must be based on capital × time invested, not capital alone.

Scan responses on the way out. Any answer that still says "because the ratio is different" without naming *time* as the missing factor is the signal to reopen this exact example at the start of Session 23.

**Homework**

1. Re-solve, from scratch and without checking the answers, all three "Quiz Time" problems from today's deck: the charity-deduction profit problem, the subscribe-capital-with-offsets problem, and the investment-ratio-from-profit-ratio problem.
2. Re-attempt the Rs.5,783 deduction problem (the one with the 4:6:9 split) independently — this is a different ratio skill from the rest of today's set and is worth a second, unaided attempt.
3. If setting up ratios quickly still feels shaky, revisit Session 21's alligation examples — the same ratio skills are load-bearing in both sessions.

> *"Tonight, before you touch any of these, write down the one rule from today in your own words: profit share depends on capital *and* time. If you can't state that rule from memory, everything else in the homework will feel like guesswork."*

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| Profit is split by capital alone, ignoring how long each amount was invested | It's the simpler rule, and it's correct whenever time happens to be equal (as in Beats 1 and 2) | Activity 2 — the Rs.40,000 vs Rs.50,000 comparison, grounded in the deck's own hint on Example 3: "profit depends on time period of investment" |
| A late-joining or part-withdrawn investment counts for the full period anyway | Students track *how much* money was put in, not *when* | Slide Block A, Beat 3 — walking through B's 6-months-then-3-months split explicitly before revealing the total |
| In a deduction-then-ratio problem, the final share is just the ratio share of the remainder | The ratio step is the "interesting" part, so the add-back step gets skipped | Activity 1, Problem 2 — catching the "forgot to add the Rs.28 back" version of the answer |
| Reversing "profit ratio → investment ratio" means multiplying by time again | The forward rule multiplies, so reversing feels like it should too | Slide Block B's live verification step — dividing profit ratio by time, then checking by multiplying back |
| The full profit is always split among the named partners | Charity/other deductions aren't mentioned unless the problem states them, so it's easy to assume 100% is shared | Classroom Quiz Q1 (deck slide 11) — walking through "only 95% is shared" explicitly |
