## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Explain why "X% more than" and "X% less than" are not mirror images of each other, and identify which quantity is the base in each case. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
2. Solve reverse-percentage and shortfall problems (e.g. "failed by N marks", "majority of N votes") by converting a marks/vote gap into a percentage gap of the same total. *(APPLYING)*
3. Solve multi-stage "percentage of the remaining" expenditure problems by working off the balance left after each deduction, not off the original total. *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve problems where a percentage price change causes a proportional change in quantity, without assuming the quantity changes by the same percentage as the price. *(APPLYING)*
5. Compute a combined/weighted percentage across two sub-groups of different sizes (e.g. male and female students) by finding sub-group counts first, then combining. *(ANALYZING)*
6. Translate "ratio + percentage" word problems (e.g. rows vs. columns, one person's marks as a % of a total) into a single solvable equation. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice (0–7 min)

7 questions on **Session 11** (Percentage Change & Successive Increment/Decrement). Newly authored, grounded in Session 11's real deck numbers. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** In plain terms, "percentage change" is calculated as:
`A` new value alone · `B` (change ÷ original) × 100 · `C` (change ÷ new value) × 100 · `D` not sure
→ **B.** *Targets:* the base of a percentage-change calculation is always the *original* value — the fact today's entire session leans on.

**Q2.** For two successive increases of a% and b%, the shortcut net % change is:
`A` a + b · `B` a + b + ab/100 · `C` (a + b) / 2 · `D` a × b / 100
→ **B.** *Targets:* the "conventional vs. shortcut" method Session 11 taught for successive increment/decrement.

**Q3.** Sai starts on Rs. 20,000 and gets a 10% hike every year. What is his salary after the **first** year?
`A` 20,500 · `B` 21,000 · `C` 22,000 · `D` 24,200
→ **C.** *Targets:* applying one successive hike before chaining a second. (20,000 × 1.10 = 22,000; deck confirms year-2 salary of 24,200.)

**Q4.** After Sai's two successive 10% annual hikes on Rs. 20,000, his overall percentage increase is:
`A` 20% · `B` 21% · `C` 10% · `D` 24.2%
→ **B — 21%.** *Targets:* the deck's own stated answer (Rs. 24,200, 21%). *If most pick A:* they added 10+10 instead of compounding — flag it, Activity content today assumes they don't make this mistake again.

**Q5.** Lohitha starts on Rs. 30,000. She gets +20% in year one and −10% in year two. The net change in her salary is:
`A` 10% increase · `B` 8% increase · `C` 8% decrease · `D` 10% decrease
→ **B.** *Targets:* successive change with a mixed increase/decrease (deck states 8% increase).

**Q6.** Pavan's salary is 40% more than Prudhvi's. Prudhvi's salary is what % less than Pavan's?
`A` 40% · `B` 60% · `C` 28.57% · `D` 25%
→ **C — 28.57%** (deck: 28.5714). *If most pick A:* this is exactly the base-switching trap Objective 1 targets — flag the number, revisit it explicitly in the Hook.

**Q7.** The price of dal rises by 10%. To keep expenditure unchanged, consumption must be reduced by:
`A` 10% · `B` 11% · `C` 9 1/11% · `D` 9%
→ **C — 9 1/11%** (deck's own answer). *Targets:* the "reduce consumption to offset a price rise" pattern — a close cousin of today's egg-price problem in Activity 2.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question, project the distribution after each. Total 7 min including your reads.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"Percentages 1 gave you the tools. Today gives you no new formula at all — not one. Today is nine real exam-style questions, back to back, and the only skill being tested is: which tool do you reach for, and how fast."*

Put this on the board, nothing else (from the deck's own opening problem):

> *In an examination, X secures 58% of marks and Y secures 105 marks less than X. If the maximum marks were 700, then how much % of marks did Y secure?*

Ask: *"Thirty seconds, no calculators, gut instinct only — shout out a rough answer or a first move."*

Take 2–3 shouted first moves. Some will try to work in absolute marks the whole way through. Then say:

> *"Here's the one habit that makes this whole session fast: the moment you see a gap in *marks* and a percentage already sitting on the same total, convert the marks gap into a percentage gap immediately. 105 out of 700 is 15%. X had 58%. Y has 58 − 15 = 43%. That's it. No long division of Y's actual marks needed."*

Tie back to **Q6** of the poll — *"That Pavan/Prudhvi question a minute ago was the opposite trap — same idea, different direction. Today you'll meet both directions, repeatedly."*

## Slide Block A (10–22 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred from deck order (slides 4–6); confirm against live deck -->
Covers: the X/Y marks problem → the pass-mark shortfall problem → Dheeraj's multi-stage expenditure problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Beat 1 — X & Y marks (slide 4).** Convert the absolute marks gap (105) into a % of the same total (700) *first* — 105/700 = 15%. Then it's simple subtraction from X's known 58%. This is the technique from the Hook; land it formally here.
- **Beat 2 — Pass-mark shortfall (slide 5).** *"A student gets 198 marks and is declared failed by 36 marks"* means the passing mark itself is 198 + 36 = 234. Passing mark is 36% of the maximum, so 0.36 × Max = 234 → Max = 650. The phrase "failed by N" always means *add back*, never subtract.
- **Beat 3 — Dheeraj's expenditure (slide 6).** 30% of income goes to petrol; petrol = 300, so income = 300 ÷ 0.30 = 1000. The **25% on rent is 25% of the remaining balance (700), not of the original 1000.** Rent = 0.25 × 700 = 175. This is Session 11's "percentage of the remaining" idea, now dressed as money instead of speed/water content.

**Checkpoint (at 22 min)** — cold-call two students:
> *"In the Dheeraj problem: what was his income, and what balance was rent calculated on?"*
> **Answer:** Income = Rs. 1000 (300 ÷ 0.30). Petrol takes 300, leaving 700. Rent = 25% of 700 = Rs. 175 — **not** 25% of 1000.

## ⚡ Activity 1 — Think–Pair–Share (22–28 min)

### What this activity is

Two real problems from the deck (slides 7 and 8), both algebra-in-disguise. Students solve silently first, compare with a partner, and only then does the class see the worked method. No lecture-first — they attempt cold.

### Why it's here

Both problems punish "plug into a formula" thinking. They reward setting up **one clean equation** from the words. That's the exact skill Slide Block A just modelled — this activity checks whether it transferred.

### Before class

Have both problems on one slide/board, both visible at once, answers hidden.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line, reveal both problems | Read |
| 0:30–3:00 | Silent solve, then pair up | Solve individually, then compare with partner |
| 3:00–5:30 | Take the equation (not the final number) from two different pairs, work it live | Explain, watch |
| 5:30–6:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Two problems. Ninety seconds alone, then compare with your partner — if your equations don't match, figure out whose is right before I come to you. I want the equation, not the answer, when I call on you."*

**Problem 1 (slide 7):**
> The sum of the number of boys and girls in a school is 150. If the number of boys is x, then the number of girls becomes x% of the total number of students. The number of boys are?

**Problem 2 (slide 8):**
> In an election a candidate who gets 86% of the votes is elected by a majority of 576 votes. What is the total number of votes polled?

### Answers

| # | Equation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Girls = 150 − x = (x/100) × 150 → 150 − x = 1.5x → x = 60 | **60 boys** |
| 2 | Winner 86%, loser 14%, gap 72% = 576 votes → Total = 576 ÷ 0.72 | **800 votes** |

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs write "girls = x%" instead of "girls = (x/100) × 150" | They dropped the total. Ask: *"x% of what?"* — the answer must be the whole school, not x itself. |
| On the election problem, a pair uses 86% instead of the 72% gap | Ask: *"If the winner has 86%, what does the loser have — and what's the actual gap between them?"* |
| Everyone finishes early | Ask them to state, in one sentence, what "majority" means in vote-count problems. |

**Common instructor mistake:** revealing the final number before the equation. The equation is the transferable skill; the number is disposable.

**Cut rule:** If short on time, run the election problem only — it carries the "gap = difference of percentages" idea that recurs in the egg-price problem coming up in Activity 2.

## ⚡ Activity 2 — Spot the Bug (28–36 min)

### What this activity is

Two real deck problems (slides 10 and 11), each paired with an **instructor-authored wrong solution** — a plausible mistake, not a real deck answer — that students must diagnose and correct before reaching the deck's real answer.

### Why it's here

Both source problems hide a base-switching trap: a percentage change in one quantity does not translate 1-for-1 into the same percentage change in a related quantity. This is the strongest misconception in the whole session, and it's the same family as Q6 in the Warm-Up Poll.

### Before class

Have both "wrong solutions" ready to reveal on screen, after students have attempted the real problem cold.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–3:30 | Show problem 1, then the wrong solution | Diagnose the bug, then solve correctly |
| 3:30–4:00 | Reveal correct method + answer | Check |
| 4:00–7:00 | Repeat for problem 2 | Diagnose, solve, check |
| 7:00–8:00 | Debrief | Listen |

### Say this

> *"I'm going to show you a solution that looks confident and is wrong. Your job: find exactly where it breaks, in one sentence, before I show you the real method."*

**Problem 1 (slide 10):**
> Due to an increase of 30% in the price of eggs, 3 eggs less are available for Rs. 7.80/-. The present rate of eggs per dozen is?

**The bug (authored, not from the deck):** *"Price went up 30%, so the quantity you get must have dropped by 30% too. 3 eggs is 30% of the original count, so the original count was 3 ÷ 0.30 = 10 eggs."*

**Why it's wrong:** a 30% price rise does **not** shrink quantity by 30% for the same money — it shrinks it by 30/130 (≈23.08%), because the new, larger price is the base for the new smaller quantity, not the old price.

**Correct method:** let original price per egg = p. `7.80/p − 7.80/1.3p = 3` → `7.80 × (0.3/1.3) / p = 3` → `p = 0.60`. New price per egg = 1.3 × 0.60 = 0.78 → **per dozen = 0.78 × 12 = Rs. 9.36** (matches the deck).

**Problem 2 (slide 11):**
> Two students appeared at an examination. One of them secured 9 marks more than the other and his marks was 56% of the sum of their marks. The marks obtained by them are?

**The bug (authored, not from the deck):** *"9 marks is 56% of something, so the sum = 9 ÷ 0.56 ≈ 16."*

**Why it's wrong:** the 9-mark gap is *not* 56% of the sum — it's the **difference** between the two students' shares of the sum: 56% and (100 − 56) = 44%. The gap in percentage terms is 56 − 44 = 12%, not 56%.

**Correct method:** `12% of sum = 9` → `sum = 75`. Marks = 56% of 75 = **42**, and 44% of 75 = **33** — matches the deck's "33, 42".

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students defend the "30% fewer eggs" logic | Ask them to check it: does 30 eggs at old price and 30×0.7=21 at new price actually keep total spend the same? Walk the numbers — it doesn't balance until you use 30/130. |
| Nobody spots the 56%/44% gap | Ask: *"If one student has 56% of the total, what's left for the other student?"* Once they say 44%, the 12% gap is one step away. |
| Running long | Do the egg problem only — it's the stronger, more common misconception. |

**Common instructor mistake:** correcting the bug verbally without re-deriving the equation on the board. Students need to see *why* the base changed, not just be told the right number.

**Cut rule:** Egg problem only if short on time — see above.

## Slide Block B (36–44 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred from deck order (slides 12–13); confirm against live deck -->
Covers: the rows/columns algebra problem → the acoustics-class engineering-pass-rate problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Beat 1 — Rows and columns (slide 12).** *"12.5% more"* is the same as a ratio of 9:8 (12.5% = 1/8, so 12.5% more = ×9/8). Let columns-count = C, rows-count = R = 1.125C. `R × C = 72` and `R = 1.125C` → `C² × 1.125 = 72` → `C² = 64` → `C = 8, R = 9`. **Children per row = 9.** Flag the fraction-% equivalence (12.5% = 1/8) explicitly — it's a Percentages-1 fact this problem leans on hard.
- **Beat 2 — Acoustics class (slide 13).** Two sub-groups, don't average their percentages — find each sub-group's *count* first. Male engineering = 25% of 120 = 30. Female engineering = 20% of 100 = 20. Total engineering = 50. Male eng. passed = 20% of 30 = 6. Female eng. passed = 25% of 20 = 5. Total passed = 11. **% of engineering students who passed = 11/50 = 22%.**

**Checkpoint (at 44 min)** — show hands:
> *"In the acoustics problem — how many total engineering students were there, and how many of them passed?"*
> **Answer:** 50 engineering students (30 male + 20 female); 11 passed (6 male + 5 female) = 22%.

## ⚡ Activity 3 — Rapid Fire Board Race (44–52 min)

### What this activity is

Two teams race to write the **correct setup equation** (not the final answer) for the two problems just taught in Slide Block B. First team to the board with a correct equation scores the point; the other team gets a chance to challenge it before it's confirmed.

### Why it's here

This is the deck's own rhythm — a block of taught problems followed immediately by a "Quiz Time" checkpoint (slides 9 and 14 in the source deck). The source deck's own Quiz Time slides carry no extractable question text, so this activity substitutes a live, self-contained drill on the same two problems, at speed, to consolidate rather than reteach.

### Before class

Split the room into two teams. Have a stopwatch and the board split into two halves.

### Run it

| Time | You do | Students do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | Setup line | Listen |
| 0:30–4:00 | Race 1: rows/columns problem | One rep per team races to the board |
| 4:00–7:00 | Race 2: acoustics problem | One rep per team races to the board |
| 7:00–8:00 | Debrief, declare winner | Listen |

### Say this

> *"Same two problems you just watched me solve. I want the equation on the board, correctly set up, faster than the other team. Answer alone doesn't score — I need to see the setup."*

**Race 1 — rows/columns (slide 12):**
> A class of 72 children is arranged in rows and columns in such a way that the number of children in each row is 12.5% more than the number of students in each column. How many children are there in each row?
**Winning setup:** `R = 1.125 × C`, `R × C = 72` → **R = 9**

**Race 2 — acoustics class (slide 13):**
> In an acoustics class, 120 students are male and 100 students are female. 25% of the male students and 20% of the female students are engineering students. 20% of the male engineering students and 25% of the female engineering students passed the final exam. What percentage of engineering students passed the exam?
**Winning setup:** Male eng. = 0.25×120=30, Female eng. = 0.20×100=20, passed = 0.20×30 + 0.25×20 = 6+5=11, of 50 total → **22%**

### When it goes wrong

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| A team writes only the final number | No point awarded. Say why out loud: *"I can't tell if you got lucky or got it right."* |
| Both teams stall on Race 2's two sub-groups | Pause the clock, ask the room: *"How many groups do we actually need counts for before we can even talk about percentages?"* Answer: four (male eng., female eng., male eng. passed, female eng. passed). |
| One team is clearly weaker | Let the stronger team's rep explain their equation to the room after each race — that's the payoff, not just the score. |

**Common instructor mistake:** rushing to declare a winner and skipping the explanation. The race is the hook for attention; the explanation is the actual learning.

**Cut rule:** If short on time, run Race 2 only — it's the more error-prone of the two and the one most worth drilling under time pressure.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| "X% more than" and "X% less than" are the same number in reverse | The two phrases sound symmetric | Warm-Up Poll Q6 (Pavan/Prudhvi) and the Hook, both landing on the same trap from opposite directions |
| A price rising by p% causes quantity to fall by the same p%, for fixed spend | Feels proportional / intuitive | Activity 2's authored "bug" on the egg problem, then the real 30/130 derivation |
| "9 marks more, and it's 56% of the sum" means 9 = 56% of the sum | Reads the percentage as attached to the smaller number in the sentence | Activity 2's second bug — showing the gap is 56% − 44% = 12%, not 56% |
| Multi-stage expenditure/percentage problems apply every percentage to the original total | Easiest reading of the words, ignores "of the remaining" | Slide Block A, Beat 3 (Dheeraj) and its Checkpoint |
| Two sub-groups' percentages can be averaged directly to get an overall percentage | Averaging feels like the "combine" operation | Slide Block B, Beat 2 (acoustics) — sub-group counts first, always |
| "12.5% more" needs a decimal calculation | Fraction-% equivalents (1/8, 1/4, etc.) from Percentages 1 aren't yet automatic | Slide Block B, Beat 1 — flagging 12.5% = 1/8 explicitly before setting up the ratio |
