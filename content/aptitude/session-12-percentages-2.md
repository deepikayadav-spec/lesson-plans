## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

1. Explain why "X% more than" and "X% less than" are not mirror images of each other, and identify which quantity is the base in each case. *(UNDERSTANDING)*
2. Solve reverse-percentage and shortfall problems by converting a marks/vote gap into a percentage gap of the same total. *(APPLYING)*
3. Solve multi-stage "percentage of the remaining" expenditure problems by working off the balance left after each deduction, not off the original total. *(APPLYING)*
4. Solve problems where a percentage price change causes a proportional change in quantity, without assuming the quantity changes by the same percentage as the price. *(APPLYING)*
5. Compute a combined/weighted percentage across two sub-groups of different sizes by finding sub-group counts first, then combining. *(ANALYZING)*
6. Translate "ratio + percentage" word problems into a single solvable equation. *(ANALYZING)*

## Warm-Up Poll — Retrieval Practice (3–7 min) · ALS: Polling

5 questions on **Session 11** (Percentage Change & Successive Increment/Decrement). Newly authored, grounded in Session 11's real deck numbers. ~45 s each, project the distribution, never name individuals.

**Q1.** In plain terms, "percentage change" is calculated as:
`A` new value alone · `B` (change ÷ original) × 100 · `C` (change ÷ new value) × 100 · `D` not sure
→ **B.** *Targets:* the base of a percentage-change calculation is always the *original* value — the fact today's entire session leans on.

**Q2.** After Sai's two successive 10% annual hikes on Rs. 20,000, his overall percentage increase is:
`A` 20% · `B` 21% · `C` 10% · `D` 24.2%
→ **B — 21%.** *If most pick A:* they added 10+10 instead of compounding — flag it, today's activities assume they don't make this mistake again.

**Q3.** Lohitha starts on Rs. 30,000. She gets +20% in year one and −10% in year two. The net change in her salary is:
`A` 10% increase · `B` 8% increase · `C` 8% decrease · `D` 10% decrease
→ **B.** *Targets:* successive change with a mixed increase/decrease.

**Q4.** Pavan's salary is 40% more than Prudhvi's. Prudhvi's salary is what % less than Pavan's?
`A` 40% · `B` 60% · `C` 28.57% · `D` 25%
→ **C — 28.57%.** *If most pick A:* this is exactly the base-switching trap today's Hook targets directly.

**Q5.** The price of dal rises by 10%. To keep expenditure unchanged, consumption must be reduced by:
`A` 10% · `B` 11% · `C` 9 1/11% · `D` 9%
→ **C — 9 1/11%.** *Targets:* the "reduce consumption to offset a price rise" pattern — a close cousin of today's egg-price problem.

**Running it** — poll tool, ~45 s per question. Total ~3.75 min for the 5 questions.

## Hook (7–10 min)

Say: *"Percentages 1 gave you the tools. Today gives you no new formula at all — not one. Today is nine real exam-style questions, back to back, and the only skill being tested is: which tool do you reach for, and how fast."*

Put this on the board, nothing else (from the deck's own opening problem):

> *In an examination, X secures 58% of marks and Y secures 105 marks less than X. If the maximum marks were 700, then how much % of marks did Y secure?*

Ask: *"Thirty seconds, no calculators, gut instinct only — shout out a rough answer or a first move."*

Take 2–3 shouted first moves. Some will try to work in absolute marks the whole way through. Then say:

> *"Here's the one habit that makes this whole session fast: the moment you see a gap in *marks* and a percentage already sitting on the same total, convert the marks gap into a percentage gap immediately. 105 out of 700 is 15%. X had 58%. Y has 58 − 15 = 43%. That's it. No long division of Y's actual marks needed."*

Tie back to **Q4** of the poll — *"That Pavan/Prudhvi question a minute ago was the opposite trap — same idea, different direction. Today you'll meet both directions, repeatedly."*

## Slide Block A (10–17 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred from deck order (slides 4–6); confirm against live deck -->
Covers: the X/Y marks problem → the pass-mark shortfall problem → Dheeraj's multi-stage expenditure problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Beat 1 — X & Y marks (slide 4).** Convert the absolute marks gap (105) into a % of the same total (700) *first* — 105/700 = 15%. Then it's simple subtraction from X's known 58%.
- **Beat 2 — Pass-mark shortfall (slide 5).** *"A student gets 198 marks and is declared failed by 36 marks"* means the passing mark itself is 198 + 36 = 234. Passing mark is 36% of the maximum, so 0.36 × Max = 234 → Max = 650. The phrase "failed by N" always means *add back*, never subtract.
- **Beat 3 — Dheeraj's expenditure (slide 6).** 30% of income goes to petrol; petrol = 300, so income = 300 ÷ 0.30 = 1000. The **25% on rent is 25% of the remaining balance (700), not of the original 1000.** Rent = 0.25 × 700 = 175.

**Checkpoint (at 17 min)** — 10 s silent think, cold-call two students:
> *"In the Dheeraj problem: what was his income, and what balance was rent calculated on?"*
> **Answer:** Income = Rs. 1000. Petrol takes 300, leaving 700. Rent = 25% of 700 = Rs. 175 — **not** 25% of 1000.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 1 — Think-Pair-Share (17–23 min)

**ALS format:** Think-Pair-Share. Chosen right after Slide Block A because both real deck problems punish "plug into a formula" thinking and reward setting up **one clean equation** from the words — this checks whether that skill transferred, not whether the answer alone was found.

**Setup line:**
> *"Two problems. Ninety seconds alone, then compare with your partner — if your equations don't match, figure out whose is right before I come to you. I want the equation, not the answer, when I call on you."*

**Problem 1 (slide 7):**
> The sum of the number of boys and girls in a school is 150. If the number of boys is x, then the number of girls becomes x% of the total number of students. The number of boys are?

**Problem 2 (slide 8):**
> In an election a candidate who gets 86% of the votes is elected by a majority of 576 votes. What is the total number of votes polled?

**Answers**

| # | Equation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Girls = 150 − x = (x/100) × 150 → 150 − x = 1.5x → x = 60 | **60 boys** |
| 2 | Winner 86%, loser 14%, gap 72% = 576 votes → Total = 576 ÷ 0.72 | **800 votes** |

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Pairs write "girls = x%" instead of "girls = (x/100) × 150" | They dropped the total. Ask: *"x% of what?"* |
| On the election problem, a pair uses 86% instead of the 72% gap | Ask: *"If the winner has 86%, what does the loser have — and what's the actual gap between them?"* |

**Debrief line:**
> *"The equation is the transferable skill; the number is disposable."*

**Cut rule:** If short on time, run the election problem only — it carries the "gap = difference of percentages" idea that recurs in ALS Activity 2's egg-price problem.

## ⚡ ALS Activity 2 — Spot the Bug (23–31 min)

**ALS format:** Silent Diagnose, Named Reveal — two real deck problems, each paired with an **instructor-authored wrong solution** that students must diagnose and correct before reaching the deck's real answer. Chosen as the session's central activity because both source problems hide a base-switching trap — the strongest misconception in the whole session, and the same family as Warm-Up Q4.

**Setup line:**
> *"I'm going to show you a solution that looks confident and is wrong. Your job: find exactly where it breaks, in one sentence, before I show you the real method."*

**Problem 1 (slide 10):**
> Due to an increase of 30% in the price of eggs, 3 eggs less are available for Rs. 7.80/-. The present rate of eggs per dozen is?

**The bug (authored, not from the deck):** *"Price went up 30%, so the quantity you get must have dropped by 30% too. 3 eggs is 30% of the original count, so the original count was 3 ÷ 0.30 = 10 eggs."*

**Why it's wrong:** a 30% price rise does **not** shrink quantity by 30% for the same money — it shrinks it by 30/130 (≈23.08%), because the new, larger price is the base for the new smaller quantity.

**Correct method:** let original price per egg = p. `7.80/p − 7.80/1.3p = 3` → `p = 0.60`. New price per egg = 1.3 × 0.60 = 0.78 → **per dozen = 0.78 × 12 = Rs. 9.36**.

**Problem 2 (slide 11):**
> Two students appeared at an examination. One of them secured 9 marks more than the other and his marks was 56% of the sum of their marks. The marks obtained by them are?

**The bug (authored, not from the deck):** *"9 marks is 56% of something, so the sum = 9 ÷ 0.56 ≈ 16."*

**Why it's wrong:** the 9-mark gap is *not* 56% of the sum — it's the **difference** between the two students' shares of the sum: 56% and 44%. The gap in percentage terms is 12%, not 56%.

**Correct method:** `12% of sum = 9` → `sum = 75`. Marks = 56% of 75 = **42**, and 44% of 75 = **33**.

**When it goes wrong**

| If… | Do this |
|---|---|
| Students defend the "30% fewer eggs" logic | Ask them to check it: does 30 eggs at old price and 21 at new price (30×0.7) actually keep total spend the same? It doesn't balance until you use 30/130. |
| Nobody spots the 56%/44% gap | Ask: *"If one student has 56% of the total, what's left for the other student?"* Once they say 44%, the 12% gap is one step away. |

**Common instructor mistake:** correcting the bug verbally without re-deriving the equation on the board.

**Cut rule:** Egg problem only if short on time — it's the stronger, more common misconception.

## Slide Block B (31–39 min) — DELIVER SLIDES AS-IS

<!-- placement: inferred from deck order (slides 12–13); confirm against live deck -->
Covers: the rows/columns algebra problem → the acoustics-class engineering-pass-rate problem.

**Beats to emphasise**

- **Beat 1 — Rows and columns (slide 12).** *"12.5% more"* is the same as a ratio of 9:8 (12.5% = 1/8, so 12.5% more = ×9/8). Let columns-count = C, rows-count = R = 1.125C. `R × C = 72` and `R = 1.125C` → `C² = 64` → `C = 8, R = 9`. **Children per row = 9.**
- **Beat 2 — Acoustics class (slide 13).** Two sub-groups, don't average their percentages — find each sub-group's *count* first. Male engineering = 25% of 120 = 30. Female engineering = 20% of 100 = 20. Male eng. passed = 20% of 30 = 6. Female eng. passed = 25% of 20 = 5. Total passed = 11 of 50 → **22%**.

**Checkpoint (at 39 min)** — show hands:
> *"In the acoustics problem — how many total engineering students were there, and how many of them passed?"*
> **Answer:** 50 engineering students (30 male + 20 female); 11 passed = 22%.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why students hold it | Correct it live by |
|---|---|---|
| "X% more than" and "X% less than" are the same number in reverse | The two phrases sound symmetric | Warm-Up Poll Q4 (Pavan/Prudhvi) and the Hook, both landing on the same trap from opposite directions |
| A price rising by p% causes quantity to fall by the same p%, for fixed spend | Feels proportional / intuitive | ALS Activity 2's authored "bug" on the egg problem |
| "9 marks more, and it's 56% of the sum" means 9 = 56% of the sum | Reads the percentage as attached to the smaller number | ALS Activity 2's second bug — showing the gap is 56% − 44% = 12%, not 56% |
| Multi-stage expenditure/percentage problems apply every percentage to the original total | Easiest reading of the words, ignores "of the remaining" | Slide Block A, Beat 3 (Dheeraj) |
| Two sub-groups' percentages can be averaged directly to get an overall percentage | Averaging feels like the "combine" operation | Slide Block B, Beat 2 (acoustics) |
